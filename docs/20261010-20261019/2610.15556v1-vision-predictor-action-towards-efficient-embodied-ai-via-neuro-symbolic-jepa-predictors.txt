# ABSTRACT 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models commonly generate robot actions token by token through Large Language Model (LLM) decoders, so their inference latency grows with the length of the action sequence and limits the achievable closed-loop control frequency. We present the Goal-Conditioned Vision-Predictor-Action (VPA) framework, which replaces the autoregressive decoder with a lightweight latent control loop. A shared vision encoder and a frozen text encoder embed observations, goal images, and instructions; a neuro-symbolic selector chooses a discrete skill; a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) predictor forecasts the next latent state; and a flow-matching solver generates an entire action chunk in a few integration steps, so that the number of sequential network evaluations per decision step does not depend on the action horizon. We analyze the inference complexity of this design, its regularization against representation collapse, and the assumptions under which latent sub-goal thresholding can limit error accumulation across task phases. As an initial proof of concept, we implemented VPA in PyTorch and evaluated it on the LIBERO-10 long-horizon manipulation suite. The trainable model has fewer than 20 Mparameters, and each released checkpoint is under 75 MB, excluding a frozen 63 .4M-parameter CLIP text encoder; a decision step of a single-camera reference configuration takes approximately 

10 –14 ms on an Apple M4; and, trained only on the LIBERO-10 demonstrations in under three hours on a single GPU, a policy observing the scene through two cameras succeeded in 40 .0% of 200 

evaluation episodes. These results come from a single training run and remain below previously reported success rates on this suite, but they suggest that the approach is workable. Code and model weights are available at https://github.com/ylee-sling/Vision-Predictor-Action and

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in embodied artificial intelligence have been largely driven by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which extend web-scale pre-trained backbones to physical robotic manipulation [1, 2]. By mapping visual observations and natural language instructions into discrete token spaces, these systems exhibit strong semantic generalization across diverse tasks [3]. However, many current VLA architectures emit actions through autoregressive Large Language Model (LLM) decoders [4]. When continuous actions are discretized into tokens, generating an H-step action chunk of dimension da requires N = q · H · da tokens, where q denotes the number of tokens per scalar action component, and hence Θ( N ) sequential forward passes through a multi-billion-parameter Transformer per execution cycle (Section 5.1). To mitigate these latency constraints, hybrid approaches such as NS-VLA [4] introduce discrete neuro-symbolic skill pointers, while VLA-JEPA [5] incorporates joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) [6, 7] to model latent world dynamics [8, 9]. Despite these improvements, such systems remain coupled to large causal vision-language backbones (e.g., Qwen3-VL-2B) that consume substantial computational resources, and their high-level plans are expressed as predefined action symbols (e.g., pick , place_on ) that constitute the interface between reasoning and control. Concurrently, non-autoregressive trajectory generators based on Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) [10], Diffusion Policies [11], and Conditional Flow-Matching (CFM) [12, 13, 14] have demonstrated high-frequency motor execution ( 30 –100 Hz). These methods, however, typically either operate as reactive visual policies without forward-predictive dynamics or explicit sub-goal tracking, or, when language-conditioned as in π0 [14], retain a large vision-language backbone for instruction processing. In this work, we present the Goal-Conditioned Vision-Predictor-Action (VPA) framework, which replaces autore-gressive language decoding with a non-autoregressive latent control loop. As summarized in Figure 1, our reference implementation, trained only on the LIBERO-10 demonstrations, has fewer than 20 M trainable parameters (a checkpoint of under 75 MB), runs a decision step in approximately 10 –14 ms on an Apple M4 for a single-camera reference config-uration, and reaches a promising initial success rate of 40 .0% on LIBERO-10. By substituting the LLM decoder—the dominant source of sequential latency in token-based VLA models—with a lightweight JEPA predictor, VPA reduces both the parameter count and the sequential depth of the per-step computation. Rather than communicating through text tokens, the predictor supplies forecasted state-transition latents directly to the downstream action solver, which generates continuous and potentially multi-modal action distributions within the skill selected by a discrete primitive. VPA thus retains a symbolic vocabulary for high-level skill selection, but confines it to the choice of which skill is executed, while the motor trajectory itself is synthesized in a continuous space. We note that this design does not remove the symbolic bottleneck entirely: the set of high-level behaviors that VPA can compose is bounded by its primitive dictionary, and zero-shot generalization to skills outside this dictionary is not addressed by the present formulation. # 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recent advancements in embodied artificial intelligence have been largely driven by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as RT-2 [1], OpenVLA [2], and Instruct2Act [3]. These architectures extend web-scale pre-trained backbones to map visual observations and natural language instructions into multi-modal action spaces. However, VLAs that emit discretized action tokens through an autoregressive decoder incur an inherent sequential bottleneck: generating a multi-step action chunk requires N sequential forward passes through a multi-billion-parameter Transformer decoder, where N grows linearly with the action horizon, resulting in Θ( N ) sequential decoding steps per execution cycle. For RT-2, real-time inference rates of about 1–3 Hz for the 55B-parameter model and about 5 Hz for its 5B-parameter variant have been reported [1]; such rates reduce the effectiveness of autoregressive VLAs for real-time dynamic manipulation and for compensating physical perturbations. Generalist policies that replace token decoding with continuous generative action heads, such as Octo [23] and π0 [14], partially alleviate this bottleneck; we discuss these approaches in Section 2.4. 

2.2 Neuro-Symbolic Robotics 

To combine high-level semantic reasoning with continuous motor execution, neuro-symbolic robotics incorporates formal logic and discrete task structures. Classical approaches utilize Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and automata to enforce reactive mission planning and safety constraints [18]. More recently, hybrid frameworks like NS-VLA [4] introduce discrete skill pointers to decompose complex manipulation tasks into atomic primitives. Nevertheless, existing neuro-symbolic VLA architectures continue to rely on causal Vision-Language Models (VLMs) or autoregressive text parsers for instruction processing and high-level logic; consequently, they inherit the sequential inference latency bottleneck, which limits high-frequency closed-loop execution. Concurrent work has also begun to examine neuro-symbolic formulations of JEPA, including approaches that couple knowledge and rule embeddings with joint-embedding prediction [24] and hybrid symbolic-neural latent predictors [25]. Our framework builds upon the principles of Neuro-Symbolic Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (NS-JEPA) [26], replacing autoregressive language parsers with a non-autoregressive primitive selector coupled directly to a latent predictive world model. We emphasize that the symbolic component of VPA is deliberately minimal: its transition rule (Section 4.2) realizes a monotone sequential automaton over a fixed primitive dictionary rather than a general temporal-logic specification, and it therefore inherits the expressivity limits of that dictionary. 5Vision-Predictor-Action: Towards Efficient Embodied AI via Neuro-Symbolic JEPA Predictors 

2.3 Predictive World Models and Joint-Embedding Architectures 

In contrast to generative world models that reconstruct high-dimensional visual pixels, Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) [6, 7] forecast future environment dynamics directly within abstract latent spaces. Because they dispense with pixel-level reconstruction decoders, JEPAs are not required to allocate model capacity to stochastic visual nuisances such as dynamic lighting and background shifts. Recent works, including LeWorldModel [8] and hierarchical latent planning frameworks [9], construct stable, non-collapsing latent spaces for world modeling and planning. The regularizers employed in this line of work range from variance–covariance penalties such as VICReg [19] to distribution-matching objectives that encourage isotropic Gaussian embeddings, as in LeJEPA [27]. At scale, V-JEPA 2 [28] shows that an action-conditioned latent predictor, post-trained on a modest amount of robot interaction data, can support zero-shot manipulation through planning in representation space, albeit at the cost of iterative optimization over action sequences at inference time. In robotic control, joint-embedding mechanics have been applied both as predictive dynamics models and as visual feature extractors. VLA-JEPA [5] introduces a unified pretraining framework with an online, time-causal latent world model to forecast future states. Conversely, JEPA-VLA [29] argues that standard visual backbones lack temporal policy priors and employs frozen video-predictive JEPA features as plug-and-play visual representations inside existing VLA architectures via gated cross-attention. Additionally, ACT-JEPA [30] employs latent prediction to regularize continuous imitation learning policies. While these approaches enhance visual representations or latent forward modeling, they either lack explicit, deterministic state-machine mechanisms for sub-goal verification or remain coupled to latency-heavy, text-conditioned causal language backbones. 

2.4 Diffusion and Flow-Matching Policies 

To enable high-frequency continuous control, non-autoregressive trajectory generation methods have emerged as an effective alternative. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) [10] predicts multi-step continuous action trajectories 

At ∈ RH×da in a single forward pass, which reduces the effective number of decisions per episode and has been shown to mitigate compounding errors in imitation learning. Building upon this, Diffusion Policies [11] and Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) [12, 13] formulate action-chunk generation as iterative denoising or as transport along a learned ODE vector field, respectively, enabling expressive multi-modal trajectory sampling; straight or nearly straight transport paths further permit accurate integration in few steps [31]. When combined with action chunking, these continuous solvers support real-time execution rates ( 30 –100 Hz). However, they typically function either as reactive visual policies that lack long-horizon predictive dynamics, symbolic verification, or explicit sub-goal state tracking, or as action heads attached to generalist transformer backbones [23], which in the case of π0 [14] is a pre-trained vision-language model with billions of parameters. VPA adopts a CFM solver for low-level generation but conditions it on forecasted latent transitions and a lightweight text embedding, rather than on the hidden states of a large language backbone. 

# 3 Problem Formulation 

We formulate visual-goal robotic manipulation as a Goal-Conditioned Markov Decision Process (G-MDP) defined by the tuple M = ( S, G, A, P, R, γ disc ), where S represents the continuous state space, G denotes the multimodal goal space, A is the continuous action-chunk space, P : S × A → ∆( S) represents the stochastic chunk-level transition dynamics, R is the goal-conditioned (milestone-dependent) reward function, and γdisc ∈ [0 , 1) is the discount factor. We write γdisc to distinguish the discount factor from the VICReg variance margin γ introduced in Section 5.2.

# 4 The Goal-Conditioned VPA Architecture 

Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architectures that decode actions autoregressively are constrained by a sequential computing bottleneck on control frequency. To overcome this, we introduce the Goal-Conditioned Vision-Predictor-Action (VPA) framework. The architecture bypasses large language model (LLM) backbones and formulates robotic manipulation as a closed-loop latent prediction problem. The architecture decouples high-level task logic from low-level continuous motor control through a four-stage pipeline: (1) a latent multimodal goal encoder that projects raw observations and text into a compact latent space; (2) a non-autoregressive neuro-symbolic selector that discretizes task primitives; (3) a collapse-regularized latent predictor that forecasts state transitions; and (4) a continuous flow-matching solver that outputs multi-step action trajectories. The number of sequential network evaluations per decision step in this pipeline does not depend on the action horizon (Section 5.1), which keeps the per-step computation short, as reactive control requires, while retaining semantic task specification. 4.5 Representation Alignment and Solver Warm-Starting 

Before deploying end-to-end continuous action chunking, we adopt an initial alignment phase that matches the representation used by the frozen JEPA modules to the input expected by the continuous Flow-Matching Solver πlθ .Because the latent space is learned independently of the action data, its coordinates may have arbitrary offsets and a scale ¯γ⋆ that differs substantially from that of the proprioceptive and text inputs. Conditioning the vector field on unnormalized latents can produce large gradients in the early iterations of Eq. 14. After freezing, we therefore standardize the latent components of et with fixed statistics, ˜z = ( z − ¯μZ )/¯γ⋆, applied to both zt and ˆzt+1 , where ¯μZ

is the mean latent over the training data; the proprioceptive state St is standardized analogously using dataset statistics. Because the same scale ¯γ⋆ also determines τ ⋆ and normalizes σt+1 , the completion threshold, the uncertainty signal, and the solver inputs are expressed in mutually consistent units. Utilizing principles of Behavior Cloning, the solver is then warm-started on demonstration datasets: conditioning vectors et are computed by the frozen encoder and predictor along demonstration trajectories and paired with the corresponding action chunks, and vθ is trained with Eq. 14. This alignment phase is intended to prevent the low-level solver from misinterpreting the latent transitions before any subsequent fine-tuning of the vector field. # 5 Theoretical Analysis and System Properties 

This section analyzes the runtime complexity of the VPA pipeline, the properties of its regularized latent representation, and the conditions under which its discrete sub-goal mechanism limits error accumulation across task phases. We distinguish between statements that follow directly from the definitions of the architecture and its objectives (Proposi-tions 5.1 and 5.2) and statements that additionally rely on regularity properties of the learned encoder (Proposition 5.3). Because the latter properties cannot be enforced by the training objective or by architectural constraints alone, the corresponding conclusions are presented as conditional results and as empirical expectations to be verified in the evaluation of Section 6.5. 

5.1 Computational Complexity and Latency 

A primary limitation of contemporary VLA models is their reliance on autoregressive token generation for continuous control. Let H be the length of the required action chunk and da the action dimension. Token-based VLAs discretize the continuous action space and generate N = q · H · da tokens per inference cycle, where q is the number of tokens per scalar action component. For an LLM-based policy with parameters WLLM , generating N tokens requires N forward passes, each of which depends on the token produced by its predecessor and therefore cannot be executed in parallel. dmodel the hidden width. Moreover, at batch size one, each pass must read all decoder parameters from memory; for models whose parameters exceed on-chip memory, the wall-clock latency therefore satisfies

5.3 Error Accumulation and Conditional Phase-Transition Bounds 

In step-by-step imitation learning (Behavioral Cloning), policies are subject to compounding covariate shift: if the learned policy errs with probability ϵπ per step under the demonstrator’s state distribution, its expected total cost over a horizon of T steps (with per-step costs in [0 , 1] ) exceeds that of the demonstrator by at most O(T 2ϵπ ), and this quadratic dependence is tight in the worst case [35]. Why these properties are expectations rather than guarantees. The constants LE , μE , and δη are properties of the learned encoder composed with image formation, and none of them is controlled by the objective of Eq. 11. Regarding Assumption 5.1, standard dot-product self-attention is not globally Lipschitz [22], so a Vision Transformer encoder possesses no global Lipschitz bound with respect to its input unless explicit constraints are imposed. On a bounded image domain a finite constant exists, but it may be very large and is neither estimated nor controlled during training. Spectral normalization of the linear layers [21], combined with a Lipschitz-continuous attention variant [22], can bound the pixel-space Lipschitz constant Lψ of Eψ . Even then, Assumption 5.1 concerns the composition with the image-formation map I, whose local sensitivity to y can be very large at occlusion and contact events, so that LE is bounded by Lψ times the Lipschitz constant of I only where the latter is finite. Spectral normalization is not part of the base formulation; its effect on representation quality and on the threshold mechanism is a subject for empirical evaluation. Regarding Assumption 5.2, a lower bound cannot be obtained from architectural constraints of this kind at all. The encoder is designed to be non-injective with respect to pixels, being intended to be invariant to η, so the lower bound must hold with respect to y alone. It therefore depends on the data and on the learned representation, and it is violated wherever task-relevant differences are not visible in the image, for example under occlusion or object symmetry. The variance term of Eq. 18 promotes only batch-level spread and does not imply Assumption 5.2. Accordingly, we do not claim that VPA guarantees non-compounding error. We instead state the expectation that, to the extent that the learned encoder satisfies Assumptions 5.1 and 5.2 with favorable constants on the deployment distribution, discrete sub-goal thresholding limits the propagation of physical error across task phases in the manner described by Proposition 5.3. This expectation is directly testable. In simulation, where the physical state yt is available, the constants μE , LE , and δη can be estimated from the lower and upper envelopes of ∥z − z′∥2 as a function of 

∥y − y′∥2 over sampled state pairs and nuisance conditions, and the phase-onset residuals rk can be measured directly (Section 6.5). 

# 6 Empirical Evaluation 

This section reports a first empirical evaluation of the reference implementation on the LIBERO-10 benchmark [20]. The experiments are intended as a proof of concept: they test whether the pipeline of Section 4 can be trained on standard demonstration data and executed in closed loop, and they measure the effect of two design choices, the camera configuration and the executed horizon Ht. Each configuration was trained once and evaluated on 200 episodes, so that success rates carry 95% intervals of roughly ±7 percentage points, and no baselines were evaluated under the same protocol. The results should therefore be read as preliminary. The trained checkpoints, the evaluation summaries, and the code used to produce them are publicly available (Section 1.1).

6.4 Latency Benchmarks 

Section 5.1 reports per-stage latency measurements of the reference implementation on an Apple M4 (Table 1); we summarize them here in the context of the LIBERO-10 experiments. For K ∈ { 1, 2, 3} and H ≤ 64 , a complete decision step took 10 .5–14 .2 ms at batch size one, of which 6.2–9.5 ms was spent in the four networks on the dependency chain. At K = 2 and H = 16 , the values used in the LIBERO-10 experiments, the complete step took 12 .1 ms. The sequential depth was K + 3 in all measured configurations, the encoder, selector, and predictor times did not change appreciably with H, and the time of one solver evaluation remained within 10% of its value at H = 8 up to H = 64 ,consistent with Proposition 5.1. Two points limit how these figures relate to the experiments above. First, the latency benchmark used the default module sizes with a single 224 × 224 view, whereas the LIBERO-10 models used 128 × 128 frames, two views in the two-camera case, and the settings of Table 2. We have not separately benchmarked the released checkpoints, although the released benchmark script accepts the configuration of a trained model. Second, in the LIBERO-10 experiments a decision step was followed by the open-loop execution of Ht = 15 actions, so the networks were evaluated once per 

15 control steps, and the simulated episodes did not run in real time. The measured latency therefore indicates the computational headroom of the pipeline rather than a constraint that was active in these experiments. Measurements on robotic deployment hardware and under real-time closed-loop operation remain future work. 

6.5 Future Work 

The experiments above establish that the pipeline can be trained and executed in a standard benchmark setting, but they leave open several questions that bear directly on the claims of this paper. First, the LIBERO-10 results should be strengthened by following the standard protocol, with several training seeds, 50 trials per task, all LIBERO suites, and baselines evaluated under identical conditions. Initializing Eψ from a pre-trained self-supervised encoder [16], which the implementation already supports, and replacing the gripper-derived primitive labels with annotated or learned segmentations are natural next steps. Second, multi-milestone tasks ( M > 1) are required to exercise the sequencing mechanism of Section 4.2, including the per-milestone time-out deferred there. Third, the properties that motivated the design, namely reactive control under dynamic perturbations and the conditional analysis of Section 5, require environments that support such perturbations and expose the privileged physical state yt.Section 5 establishes two kinds of properties: properties that follow from the architecture and its objectives (Proposi-tions 5.1 and 5.2), and properties that hold only under regularity assumptions on the learned encoder (Proposition 5.3). The latency measurements of Section 5.1 provide initial support for the former on one hardware platform; the latter remain untested. # 7 Conclusion 

The pursuit of generalized embodied intelligence has recently been supported by the scaling of autoregressive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. However, the sequential token generation on which many of these models rely makes them challenging to deploy for the high-frequency, reactive demands of physical robotic control. In this paper, we introduced the Goal-Conditioned Vision-Predictor-Action (VPA) framework, proposing a shift from text-conditioned autoregressive decoding to latent-predictive continuous control, and implemented it as an open-source reference implementation. By decoupling high-level task logic from low-level motor execution and utilizing a lightweight frozen text encoder to capture user instructions, the VPA framework aims to retain semantic task comprehension without relying on computationally heavy Large Language Models (LLMs). Our analysis establishes three results at different levels of strength. First, formulating multi-step action chunking through Conditional Flow-Matching (CFM) reduces the number of sequential network evaluations per decision step from Θ( H · da) for autoregressive token decoding to K + 3 , a quantity independent of the action horizon H; the arithmetic cost of each solver evaluation nevertheless still scales with the chunk dimensionality H · da, so the per-cycle cost is not O(1) in H. Second, the VICReg variance and covariance penalties exclude fully collapsed representations from the global minimizers of the joint-embedding objective whenever a non-degenerate representation attains a lower objective value, and they exclude dimensional collapse on batches where both penalties vanish; the realized variance margin further provides a principled scale for calibrating the milestone completion threshold τ . Third, discrete sub-goal thresholding bounds the physical residual at each phase transition by (τ + δη )/μ E , but only under a co-Lipschitz condition on the encoder with respect to task-relevant physical state. Because Vision Transformers are not globally Lipschitz without explicit constraints, and because this lower bound cannot be enforced through architectural constraints alone, we regard the containment of error across task phases as an empirical expectation rather than a formal guarantee. 

Initial empirical validation. The reference implementation provides a first proof of concept for the framework (Section 6). The trainable modules contain fewer than 20 M parameters, and the frozen CLIP text encoder ( 63 .4Mparameters) is evaluated only once per episode, outside the per-step dependency chain. Each model trained in under three hours on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU. On an Apple M4, a complete decision step of the single-camera reference configuration took 10 .5–14 .2 ms for K ∈ { 1, 2, 3} and H ≤ 64 , and the per-stage timings were consistent with the horizon-independent sequential depth of Proposition 5.1 (Section 5.1). On LIBERO-10, with a vision encoder trained from random initialization on the demonstrations alone, the policy succeeded in 40 .0% of 200 evaluation episodes (95% interval 33 .5–46 .9% ) when observing a third-person and a wrist-mounted camera, compared with 10 .5% with the third-person camera alone. In an ablation of the executed horizon, executing nearly the full action chunk before replanning gave the best results. We regard these results as promising but preliminary: they indicate that a latent control loop without a language-model decoder can be trained from demonstrations to complete long-horizon manipulation tasks in a meaningful fraction of trials, while its per-step computation remains small enough to run at low latency on commodity hardware. 

Limitations. The repertoire of high-level behaviors is bounded by the fixed primitive dictionary U, and zero-shot synthesis of skills outside U is not addressed by the present formulation. The empirical results come from a single training run per configuration with 20 trials per task. They were not compared with baselines under the same protocol, and they remain below success rates previously reported on this suite. The experiments used a single goal image per task ( M = 1 ) and primitive labels derived heuristically from the gripper command, so the multi-milestone sequencing mechanism and the conditional analysis of Proposition 5.3 were not exercised; the encoder constants μE , LE , and δη

and the phase-onset residuals rk have not yet been measured. The uncertainty-adaptive execution horizon of Eq. 15 did not improve task success in our ablation (Section 6.3). Latency was measured for a single-camera reference configuration on one hardware platform, not for the released checkpoints or on robotic deployment hardware, and closed-loop control frequency under real-time operation was not measured. Finally, the single-frame observation of the original formulation proved insufficient in our experiments, and the reported success rate depends on extending It to two camera views. We therefore view VPA as an analytically characterized architecture with an initial working implementation, whose theoretical properties are stated together with the assumptions they require and whose empirical support is, so far, limited to the setting reported here. The next steps are to evaluate the framework under the standard LIBERO protocol with baselines, on multi-milestone tasks, and in the dynamic simulation environments outlined in Section 6.5. We expect the explicit separation between unconditional and conditional properties to provide a sound basis for this work, and we hope that the released code and checkpoints (Section 1.1) will help others reproduce, test, and improve upon these results. 24 Vision-Predictor-Action: Towards Efficient Embodied AI via Neuro-Symbolic JEPA Predictors
