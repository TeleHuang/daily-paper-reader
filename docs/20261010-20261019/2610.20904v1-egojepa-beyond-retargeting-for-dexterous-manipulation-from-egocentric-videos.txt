ABSTRACT 

Egocentric human videos offer a scalable alternative to costly robot demonstra-tions, yet transferring their manipulation priors to robot control remains chal-lenging. The commonly adopted retargeting-based approaches convert human wrist and finger poses into embodiment-specific action commands through hand-crafted geometric mappings. However, such mappings require meticulous recal-ibration across different embodiments and risk propagating hand-tracking errors into downstream robot control pipelines. To this end, we propose EgoJEPA , a three-stage framework that learns embodiment-agnostic representations from ego-centric human videos without rule-based human-to-robot retargeting: 1) Human-action pre-training learns effect-aware latent actions through JEPA-style visual prediction conditioned on hand-motion differences; 2) Latent-action-based world modeling jointly predicts future visual dynamics and latent actions, which decou-ples human prior pre-training from robot-specific control parameterizations; 3) 

Embodiment-specific tuning adapts the pretrained model to diverse robots through lightweight action adapters. Experiments on five real-world manipulation tasks across three robot embodiments demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of EgoJEPA. It achieves a 74% average success rate under the out-of-domain evalu-ation, compared with 65% for the retargeting baseline, while reducing cumulative cross-embodiment wall-clock cost by 47.2%, from 743.2 to 392.7 hours. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Despite recent advances in robotic manipulation policies (Chi et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024), scaling such systems remains constrained by their reliance on task-specific robot demonstrations, typically obtained through teleoperation or specialized capture systems (Zhao et al., 2023; Khazatsky et al., 2024; Chi et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Collecting these demonstrations is costly, labor-intensive, and time-consuming, posing a significant bottleneck to scalable robot learning. In contrast, egocentric human videos provide a scalable supervision source for transferring manipulation skills from human hand-object interactions to robotic systems (Ma et al., 2026b; Feng et al., 2026). Earlier action-free methods operate without explicit hand-pose annotations, instead learning latent action representations directly from inter-frame visual dynamics (Ye et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Bjorck et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024). However, the resulting represen-tations may capture generic visual variations rather than action-induced physical effects, limiting their interpretability and transferability to robot control. To move beyond learning solely from vi-sual supervision, recent approaches explicitly leverage human hand-pose annotations and adopt a 

retargeting-based pipeline (Kareer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026c). Specifically, human hand motions, such as wrist and finger poses, are captured using specialized devices ( e.g ., Leap Motion (Weichert et al., 2013) or Aria glasses (Engel et al., 2023)) and subsequently mapped to a target robot action space through hand-crafted rules for policy pre-training or human-robot co-training.

Despite the intuitive formulation, the retargeting pipeline suffers from two key limitations: 1) 

Embodiment-specific mapping . Retargeting relies on low-level geometric or kinematic mappings tailored to a particular robot embodiment, rather than learning transferable action semantics. Conse-quently, such mappings are tightly coupled to the robot morphology and typically require meticulous recalibration whenever either changes. As shown in Figure 1 (a), human–robot calibration alone takes 52.1 hours, accounting for 25.2% of the total pipeline time. Figure 1 (b) shows that this cost compounds when scaling to multiple robots, i.e ., retargeting must repeat the embodiment-specific human-robot calibration and pre-training whenever the robot morphology changes, which causes the cumulative wall-clock time to grow rapidly. 2) Noise propagation . Retargeting is highly sensitive to errors in estimated human motion. Hand-pose estimation noise caused by occlusions, viewpoint variations, or temporal jitter can propagate through the retargeting process, resulting in physically infeasible robot actions. As a concrete stress test, we inject Gaussian noise into human action an-notations while keeping robot training unchanged. As shown in Figure 6 (a), under a mild per-axis position noise of σ = 10 mm, the retargeting pipeline on the task of bottle cap unscrewing 

drops drastically from 60% to 30% success rate, showing that annotation errors are directly amplified into downstream robot control failures. To this end, we propose EgoJEPA , an Ego centric instantiation of the Joint Embedding Predictive 

Architecture (Assran et al., 2023) that learns embodiment-agnostic latent action representations from human videos. As shown in Figure 1, EgoJEPA comprises three stages: 1) Human-action pre-training learns generic effect-aware latent actions through human-motion-conditioned visual prediction; 2) Latent-action-based world modeling jointly predicts future visual dynamics and the 

latent actions learned in Stage 1 via joint flow matching, rather than embodiment-specific robot commands; and 3) Embodiment-specific tuning adapts the pre-trained world model to robot action spaces through lightweight adapters. Evaluated on five real-world manipulation tasks across three robot embodiments (two dexterous hands and a gripper), EgoJEPA achieves the highest average suc-cess rate of 74% under the out-of-domain evaluation, outperforming the action-free method LAPA (Ye et al., 2025), the retargeting pipeline, and the prior egocentric human-video method DexWild (Tao et al., 2025). In addition, EgoJEPA significantly reduces the cost of scaling across robot em-bodiments. By reusing Stage 1–2 models and adapting only Stage 3 for each new robot, EgoJEPA reduces cumulative wall-clock cost from 743.2 to 392.7 hours over retargeting ( c.f . Figure 1 (b)). Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose EgoJEPA, a JEPA-style framework that eliminates the need for rule-based human-to-robot retargeting by learning embodiment-agnostic representations from human videos. • We introduce effect-aware latent actions as a transferable interface between human motion priors and robot control, enabling efficient policy adaptation across diverse embodiments via lightweight action adapters. • Extensive experiments on five real-world manipulation tasks across three robot embodiments demonstrate the effectiveness and cross-embodiment scalability of EgoJEPA. 22 RELATED WORK 

WAMs for Dexterous Manipulation. WAMs move beyond action-only policies by incorporat-ing future state prediction into the action learning process, thereby modeling the coupled evolu-tion of visual dynamics and action control (Wang et al., 2026a; Shen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). While early WAMs mainly target low-DoF gripper manipulation, recent works have extended this paradigm toward dexterous manipulation (Goswami et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Jiang et al.). DexWM (Goswami et al., 2025) conditions latent-state prediction on fine-grained hand keypoint displacements for dexterous interaction modeling. DWM (Kim et al., 2026) simulates dexterous behaviors using scene-action-conditioned video diffusion without explicitly decoding robot actions. However, existing dexterous WAMs rely on embodiment-specific robot demonstrations, limiting cross-embodiment transfer and scalability. EgoJEPA instead learns transferable manipulation priors from egocentric human videos, enabling efficient adaptation to diverse robots. 

Robot Learning from Human Videos. Due to the high cost of teleoperated robot demonstrations, recent work has increasingly turned to human-centric robot learning, which leverages abundant hu-man videos as scalable semantic and physical priors (Ma et al., 2026b; Feng et al., 2026). Existing approaches can be broadly grouped into two categories. 1) Latent-based methods learn action-related representations from action-free videos by encoding inter-frame changes (Ye et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Bjorck et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026) or future visual dynamics (Wu et al., 2024; Cheang et al., 2024; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024). However, lacking explicit action grounding, such latents may conflate controllable hand motion with camera motion and passive scene dynam-ics, making them less interpretable as action representations. 2) Retargeting-based methods recover explicit human motion, e.g ., wrist poses and finger poses captured by devices like Leap Motion or Aria glasses, and map them to robot-specific action spaces for pre-training or co-training (Ka-reer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026c; Zheng et al., 2026). Although these approaches provide more structured supervision, they remain tied to embodiment-specific geometric mappings and are sensitive to hand-tracking noise. To this end, EgoJEPA encodes explicit human hand motions into effect-aware latent actions, enabling transfer to robot control without rule-based retargeting while improving robustness to hand-tracking noise. While concurrent work such as Being-H0 (Luo et al., 2025) adopts a similar spirit by discretizing MANO trajectories into motion tokens via a VQ-VAE structure, its motion tokens rely exclusively on isolated hand-pose sequences without grounding the tokenizer in the visual context. Conse-quently, such tokenizers are agnostic to visual interaction semantics, failing to capture the critical contact dynamics and spatial alignment between hands and target objects. 

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures. JEPAs formulate latent world modeling by predicting the future evolution of a system in a compact representation space rather than reconstructing raw ob-servations (LeCun et al., 2022). Early works apply JEPA to self-supervised representation learning, where models learn semantic representations by predicting latent embeddings of masked image or video patches (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025). Recent studies further explore JEPA for action-conditioned latent world modeling (Assran et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024). LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026) extends JEPA into an end-to-end action-conditioned world model via SIGReg regularization (Bardes et al., 2021). DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024) instead leverages pretrained visual representations to learn latent dynamics by predicting future semantic features. Following this spirit, EgoJEPA investigates how human motion can serve as a physical condition for latent world modeling, which enables adaptation to diverse robot embodiments.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Human Pre-training Data. We use a commercially acquired egocentric human dataset for stages 1 and 2. The dataset contains 1,111,943 episodes from 62,518 recording sessions, amounting to ap-proximately 20,106.6 hours and 2.17 billion video frames. It covers 64,686 task instructions and 608 scene labels across diverse household and industrial activities. Each episode consists of a monocular 5RGB video recorded at approximately 30Hz, together with camera calibration and motion annota-tions comprising wrist poses and 21 three-dimensional keypoints for each hand. 

Robot Post-training Tasks. We evaluate EgoJEPA on five real-world manipulation tasks spanning three robot embodiments including a Sharpa dexterous hand (Sharpa), a Wuji dexterous hand (Wuji Technology), and a Xense Tac UMI gripper (Xense Robotics). Each task is associated with a task-specific robot demonstration dataset for Stage 3 post-training: 1) Toy grasping uses the Sharpa hand to grasp a plush toy; 2) Cup stacking uses the Wuji hand to arrange cups into a target stack, requiring sequential manipulation and precise placement; 3) Zipper pulling uses the Wuji hand to pinch and slide a zipper pull; 4) Bottle cap unscrewing uses the Wuji hand to uncap a bottle through coordinated grasping; 5) Cardboard box folding uses the Xense Tac UMI gripper to fold a paper box into a target configuration, requiring multi-step manipulation. See Appendix A.1 for detailed specifications on both human pre-training and robot post-training datasets. 

Implementation Details. In Stage 1, the context and target visual encoders are initialized from the pretrained Wan2.2 VAE encoder (Wan et al., 2025), and the predictor is a 24-layer Transformer with frame-causal attention. In Stage 2, the video Transformer is initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (Wan et al., 2025), while the Stage 1 action embeddings u1 are average-pooled along the feature dimension to obtain 128-dimensional latent action targets for stable training. In Stage 3, the input and output action adapters each comprise a two-layer MLP with a ReLU activation between the layers. Full training configurations are provided in Table A3. 

Evaluation Protocol. We evaluate all policies under both in-domain and out-of-domain settings across five tasks and three robot embodiments. In-domain evaluation uses the same object instances and spatial layouts as Stage 3 training. Out-of-domain evaluation considers two forms of distribu-tion shift: 1) object shifts , using unseen object instances with different shapes, sizes, or materials while preserving task requirements; 2) spatial shifts , placing objects at positions and orientations outside the Stage 3 training distribution but within the robot’s reachable workspace. All policies are evaluated without additional fine-tuning under matched test configurations. We conduct 20 trials per task for each setting and define success as full task completion. 

Compared Methods. We evaluate EgoJEPA against five competitive methods and architectural variants: 1) LAPA (Ye et al., 2025), an action-free method that learns discrete latent actions directly from inter-frame visual changes for policy pre-training; 2) DexWild (Tao et al., 2025), an egocen-tric human-video method that co-trains dexterous manipulation policies on in-the-wild videos and robot demonstrations; 3) Retargeting-based pipeline , which shares our architecture but replaces the learned latent actions with embodiment-specific robot targets derived via geometric retargeting, fol-lowed by fine-tuning on robot data; 4) EgoJEPA (Robot-only) , an ablation that omits Stage 1–2 human-video pre-training and is trained strictly on robot demonstrations; and 5) EgoJEPA (VQ-VAE) , a variant that replaces Stage 1 JEPA pre-training with a VQ-VAE reconstruction objective (Van Den Oord et al., 2017) over hand-motion annotations, while maintaining identical Stage 2–3 protocols. Refer to Appendix A.2 for more implementation details. 4.2 REAL -W ORLD PERFORMANCE

EgoJEPA attains the highest success rate on all five tasks, with average success rates of 94% and 74% under in- and out-of-domain settings, respectively, sur-passing the retargeting pipeline by 14% and 9% absolute margins. 2) Generalization gap. All meth-ods exhibit lower average success rates under out-of-domain evaluation, highlighting the challenges posed by unseen object instances and spatial layouts. Bottle cap unscrewing and cardboard box folding yield EgoJEPA’s lowest out-of-domain success rates. JEPA vs. VQ-VAE. To assess the benefit of JEPA-based predictive learning, we compare EgoJEPA with EgoJEPA (VQ-VAE), which replaces Stage 1 predictive learning with VQ-VAE-based action reconstruction (Van Den Oord et al., 2017), using the same data and downstream training protocol. As shown in Figure 3, EgoJEPA increases the average out-of-domain success rate from 63% to 74%, with gains across all five tasks. These results suggest that learning the visual consequences of human motion yields more effective representations for downstream control than reconstructing motion alone. Refer to Appendix A.2 for more details. 

Robot Data Efficiency. We evaluate data efficiency on bottle cap unscrewing using 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the robot demonstration dataset during adaptation. As shown in Figure 5 (right), EgoJEPA consistently outperforms both robot-only training ( i.e ., EgoJEPA (Robot-only)) and the retargeting baseline across all data budgets. Notably, with only 10% of the demonstrations, EgoJEPA achieves a 35% success rate, surpassing the 30% attained by the robot-only model trained on the full dataset (100%). Similarly, using just 50% of the demonstrations, EgoJEPA reaches a 65% success rate, outperforming retargeting using all demonstrations (60%). These results demon-strate that pre-trained effect-aware representations enable substantially more data-efficient policy adaptation, significantly reducing reliance on costly robot teleoperation data.

We introduced EgoJEPA, a three-stage framework that transfers manipulation priors from egocen-tric human videos to robot control without rule-based retargeting. By grounding latent actions in visual effects and integrating them into world modeling, EgoJEPA decouples human pre-training from embodiment-specific control, enabling shared pretrained models to be reused across robots through lightweight action adapters. Experiments across five real-world tasks and three embod-iments demonstrate that EgoJEPA outperforms the retargeting pipeline by an absolute margin of 9% under out-of-domain evaluation while reducing cumulative cross-embodiment training time by 47.2%. In addition, EgoJEPA exhibits greater robustness to human annotation noise, supporting scalable learning from imperfectly annotated egocentric videos. AI USE STATEMENT

A.4 BROADER IMPACT AND LIMITATIONS 

Broader Impact. This work advances generalist robot learning by presenting an embodiment-agnostic framework that effectively leverages large-scale, low-cost human egocentric videos. By bypassing tedious manual geometric retargeting and repeated human-robot calibrations, EgoJEPA significantly lowers the computational and labor barriers to deploying multi-embodiment dexterous robotic systems in real-world environments, such as domestic assistance and industrial automation. However, scaling learning from uncurated video datasets carries the risk of inheriting or amplify-ing biases present in human demonstration environments. Furthermore, as autonomous dexterous manipulation systems become more capable in physical deployment, safety, alignment, and strict physical containment protocols must be rigorously established to prevent unintended hardware col-lisions or operational hazards. 

Limitations. Although EgoJEPA demonstrates strong generalization and cross-embodiment effi-ciency for dexterous manipulation, several limitations remain: (1) Performance bounds under severe action quality issues: While EgoJEPA exhibits noise robustness, extreme degradation in egocentric video actions ( e.g ., severe temporal misalignments or prolonged occlusions) can still distort latent representations and bottleneck downstream task performance. (2) Contact-intensive dynamics: Per-formance drops on out-of-domain tasks that require fine-grained contact dynamics ( e.g ., bottle cap unscrewing ). Integrating explicit interaction modeling or force-feedback priors into the latent action space remains a key direction for future work. 18
