Abstract: Achieving complex, long-horizon robot behavior remains challenging
because successful execution depends on skills grounded in a robot’s physical af-
fordances. Existing training-task distributions are often handcrafted or sampled
from large language models (LLMs), relying on semantic plausibility rather than
embodiment-specific feasibility. This mismatch can produce curricula that are too
difficult, redundant, or blind to non-obvious but learnable behaviors. We propose
Generative Flow on Symbolic Abstractions (FOSA), a framework for discovering
embodiment-specific capabilities by learning a grounded task generator. FOSA
parameterizes an abstract policy as a Generative Flow Network that constructs
symbolic task skeletons. Each skeleton is compiled into a simulation environment,
where low-level policy-learning performance provides the reward signal for the
abstract policy. Across heterogeneous robot platforms, FOSA discovers diverse
and executable tasks near the robot’s empirical capability frontier. After distill-
ing the discovered task policies into a single language-conditioned policy, FOSA
improves performance on unseen complex and long-horizon tasks. The code and
demonstration videos are available at https://www.youwei-yu.com/fosa.

Introduction
Robot learning has become increasingly effective once the target task is specified and lies within the
robot’s learnable regime. With a well-specified reward and simulator, reinforcement learning (RL)
with domain and dynamics randomization can produce agile policies that transfer from simulation
to hardware [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. With demonstrations, expressive policy classes can represent multimodal

behaviors in contact-rich manipulation [6]. These successes still presuppose that the task distribution
has been chosen. In practice, robot embodiments are usually designed for broad classes of use rather
than a single task, leaving their actual capability space only partially explored.
The choice of task distribution becomes critical for complex and long-horizon policy learning. Skill
composition, hierarchical RL, and task-and-motion planning all rely on intermediate capabilities that
can be invoked, chained, or refined [7, 8, 9, 10]. Yet these capabilities are often assumed known,
manually designed, or proposed from human semantic intuition. These human-specified structures
are brittle across embodiments: a task that appears simple may be physically difficult for a robot,
while a non-obvious behavior may be easy to learn and useful for composition [11].
We study embodiment capability discovery: finding the task structures that a specific robot can
learn under a fixed training budget. The central object is the robot’s empirical capability frontier,
consisting of tasks that are learnable but not already trivial. This frontier is useful for two reasons.
First, frontier tasks expose skills that can support interpolation, composition, and downstream long-
horizon generalization. Second, the full task space is combinatorial, so exhaustive enumeration is
infeasible; efficient discovery requires learning where useful capabilities are likely to emerge.
We propose Generative Flow on Symbolic Abstractions (FOSA), a framework that learns an abstract
task generator over symbolic task skeletons. FOSA samples a skeleton, compiles it into a simulation
environment, trains a low-level task policy, and uses the resulting performance to update the gen-
erator. We instantiate the generator as a Generative Flow Network [12], enabling diverse sampling
over variable-length and variable-dimensional task specifications near the capability frontier. Lan-
guage models are used as compilation assistants for converting symbolic skeletons into executable
environments. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose FOSA, a general framework for embodiment capability discovery that learns an ab-
stract policy over symbolic task skeletons and grounds task selection in low-level policy-learning
performance.
• We instantiate the abstract policy with a Generative Flow Network, enabling diverse search over
variable-length and variable-dimensional task specifications near the robot’s empirical capability
frontier.
• We evaluate FOSA across heterogeneous robot platforms in simulation and the real world. We
find that proximity to the discovered capability frontier correlates with generalization to held-
out complex and long-horizon tasks, and that FOSA improves performance compared with task-
generation and curriculum-learning baselines.

Related Work
Complex and Long-Horizon Robot Behavior. Complex robot policy often requires temporal ab-
straction, reusable skills, and composition over extended horizons. Hierarchical RL and options pro-
vide classical mechanisms for learning and invoking temporally extended actions [7, 8, 13], while
skill-prior and skill-chaining methods improve long-horizon control by reusing learned behavioral
primitives [14, 15, 16, 17]. Task-and-motion planning and bilevel formulations similarly decompose
long-horizon problems into symbolic decisions and continuous control problems [9, 10]. In robotics,
platform-specific learning has produced increasingly rich behaviors, including agile quadruped loco-
motion, dexterous manipulation, and articulated-object interaction [4, 18, 19, 20, 21]. These works
demonstrate the importance of skills and compositional structure, while typically assuming that the
relevant task family, skill library, or decomposition is already specified.
Skill Discovery and Open-Ended Curricula. A complementary line of work studies how agents
can acquire skills, goals, or curricula with less manual supervision. Unsupervised skill discov-
ery learns diverse behaviors through intrinsic objectives based on mutual information, state cov-
erage, empowerment, contrastive representation learning, or predictable skill-conditioned dynam-
ics [22, 23, 24, 25]. Goal-generation and curriculum-learning methods adapt the training distribu-
tion according to learning progress, regret, success rate, or task difficulty [26, 27, 28]. Open-ended
2


environment design further studies how to generate environments that induce increasingly complex
agent behavior [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. These methods reduce manual ordering of experience, but
often operate within a fixed task family, fixed simulator parameterization, or motor-level behav-
ior space. FOSA instead searches over symbolic task skeletons and scores each candidate through
embodiment-specific policy learning.
Language-Driven Generation and Scalable Robot Learning. Large language models have re-
cently been used to scale robot learning by generating tasks, rewards, curricula, code, simulation
scenes, and supervision [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. These systems reduce manual engineer-
ing and make it possible to instantiate more diverse robot-learning problems. In parallel, sim-to-real
methods improve transfer through domain randomization, dynamics randomization, system identi-
fication, and online adaptation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Recent robot foundation models and vision-language-
action policies scale policy learning with large heterogeneous robot datasets [43, 44, 45, 46], while
morphology-conditioned control and robot-design methods show that policy learning is strongly
coupled to embodiment [47, 48, 49]. FOSA uses the LLM only to compile skeletons and propose
edits; task retention is decided by embodiment-grounded policy learning.

Preliminaries
We formulate robot capability discovery as learning an abstract policy over symbolic task skeletons
and grounding each sampled skeleton through low-level policy learning. We first define the task
abstraction used by FOSA, then review the GFlowNet objective used to sample diverse skeletons in
proportion to embodiment-grounded utility.
Symbolic Abstractions. We represent each candidate robot-learning problem as a symbolic task
skeleton, following the spirit of task-and-motion planning (TAMP) [9, 10]. The symbolic level
specifies objects, goals, and operators, while the continuous level specifies the geometric, dynamical,
and simulator parameters required for execution. Let Y denote entity types, P predicate schemas, U
operator schemas, and Φ feasibility constraints. For a finite set of typed entities E, let Atoms(P, E)
and Lits(P, E) denote the induced ground atoms and literals. A task skeleton is defined as

with task policies optimized in generated environments. Given a robot embodiment e, a symbolic
domain (Y, P, U, Φ), a compiler Env(·), and a fixed evaluation budget, FOSA returns an archive
of discovered skeletons and trained policies. The archive is subsequently distilled into a general
language-conditioned policy.

4.1
FOSA Framework
At each discovery iteration, the abstract policy samples a construction trajectory from x0 = ∅to
a terminal skeleton x. Deterministic validity and completeness checks reject skeletons that violate
grammar, simulator, or embodiment constraints. The compiler instantiates Me,x = Env(e, x). A
task policy πx is trained from scratch in this environment using PPO [51]; the success rate of the first
evaluation is J 0
x and that of the last is Jx. FOSA converts them into R(x), updates the GFlowNet
via Eq. (5), and adds non-duplicate successful skeletons to the archive.
The LLM enters FOSA in two roles, neither of which selects tasks. Inside Env(·) it is a compiler:
from the verbalized and serialized skeleton it writes a staged JSON specification (e.g., simulation
contract and reward) [36, 37, 40]. During sampling, it also supplies a prior over valid edits. Which
skeletons are retained and reinforced is decided solely by R(x), so FOSA is grounded by physical
policy-learning performance rather than by semantic plausibility.
4.2
FOSA Abstract Policy
The abstract policy must generate variable-dimensional task specifications through variable-length
construction sequences, since different embodiments require different entities, constraints, goals,
and arguments. FOSA parameterizes this policy as a GFlowNet and trains it with local-search
sampling [52]. Every complete skeleton has exactly one goal atom, which defines its goal family
g(x); FOSA learns one log Zg per family in Eq. (5), so that within each family terminal skeletons
are sampled in proportion to R(x) rather than collapsing onto a single best task.

Abstract State. An abstract state at construction step t is a partial skeleton
xt = [Ext, Ixt, Gxt, Uxt, θxt, Φxt].
(6)
FOSA evaluates two deterministic predicates over this structure.
The validity predicate
Valide,M(xt) checks type consistency, asset support, robot compatibility, and admissible parameter
ranges. The completeness predicate Completee,M(xt) checks whether all entities, goals, success
predicates, reward terms, and continuous arguments required for environment compilation are spec-
ified. A skeleton is complete when it binds a goal, an entity or environment, and a constraint in
at least four bindings with all required arguments set. A maximum horizon of Tmax edits prevents
redundant or circular task descriptions. PF and PB read an LLM embedding of the serialized partial
skeleton, including its arguments.
Abstract Action. An abstract action at is a symbolic edit that maps xt to xt+1. Invalid edits
are masked before sampling from PF (xt+1 | xt). The action space contains four edit classes:
add atom, which appends a typed atom (capability, entity or environment, goal, or constraint) with
default arguments; compose, which adds a composition atom that binds existing components, once
the skeleton has four bindings; refine arg, which moves one numeric argument of θxt by one of
five evenly spaced levels between its bounds, or sets a categorical argument to another admissible
value, once the skeleton is complete; and stop, which terminates the trajectory. The stop action is
enabled only when Completee,M(xt) = 1.

Running Example. For a quadruped with x0 = ∅, the abstract policy may add a robot entity to Ex,
add an inclined ramp asset, compose it with a low friction constraint in Φx, refine θx to a 20◦
incline, and terminate with a track velocity goal in Gx. The task is compiled into a slippery-
slope environment, a PPO policy is trained, and the resulting Jx updates the abstract policy.
5
Experimental Results
We evaluate FOSA on four heterogeneous robots in simulation and the real world: a ClearPath Jackal
vehicle, an Inspire Dexterous hand, a Unitree Go2 quadruped, and a Booster T1 humanoid.
Under a fixed discovery budget, each method trains 256 tasks per robot in one discovery run, and
the resulting task policies are distilled into a single language-conditioned policy. All methods use
Gemma-4-31B-it for task verbalization and environment compilation, mujoco-playground [55]
for simulation, and PPO [51] for task policies. The abstract reward uses β = 8, λnov = 0.4,
λcost = 0.05, novelty weights (watom, wpair, wsig) = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2), and a near-duplicate threshold
of 0.85 atom-set Jaccard similarity; further details are in the supplementary material.
Research Questions and Metrics. [Q1] Does FOSA discover a lexically diverse task distribution?
Div-SB4 and Div-SB5 are 1 −Self-BLEU over generated task descriptions [37, 56]; the 4-gram
variant penalizes repeated phrases and the 5-gram variant repeated task templates, so higher is more
diverse. [Q2] Does FOSA cover a broad semantic space? Dist-Embed1 and Dist-Embed2 measure
word- and phrase-level dispersion in the language-embedding space [37, 56], and Expl-Eff is the
fraction of candidates that yield executable policy-training runs. [Q3] Does FOSA generate tasks
near each embodiment’s empirical capability frontier? Frontier-Prox is the mean F(x) (Eq. (8))
over generated tasks. [Q4] Does the discovered distribution improve downstream generalization?
We measure the success and normalized episodic reward (shaped return divided by a task-specific
scale) of the distilled policy on unseen tasks.
Held-Out Tasks. For each robot we author four held-out tasks with the LLM, disjoint from every
method’s discovery log (Fig. 3): uphill climb, ice patch crossing, gravel reverse, and tight turn
(vehicle); large sphere lift, cuboid turn (counterclockwise), palm ball balance, and cylinder tip
(hand); stair descent, weak front legs, shove walk, and ice stop (quadruped); push stand, gentle
ramp up, single shove recovery, and crouch hold (humanoid).

LLM curriculum that proposes each next task from the task history [37, 40, 41]. LLM-MCTS
branches a Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) with LLM-generated symbolic expansions [57]; MCTS
removes the LLM and expands directly from the symbolic abstraction library; MCTS-Learn adds
MuZero-style learned representation, dynamics, reward, policy, and value models [58]. FOSA-RND

5.1
Simulation Results
Can FOSA discover diverse tasks efficiently? Fig. 4 compares discovered tasks across diversity,
semantic coverage, exploration efficiency, and frontier proximity. Under the same 256-task budget,
FOSA has the strongest overall profile on all robots. Its gains over the MCTS variants indicate that a
learned stochastic construction policy is more effective than expanding a tree with hand-specified or
value-estimated branching, and its gains over the LLM-driven baselines indicate that language-level
plausibility is insufficient: generated tasks must also be grounded in whether the embodiment can
learn them under a fixed policy-training budget. All LLM-driven baselines use one fixed model; a
stronger language prior would complement, not replace, embodiment-grounded scoring.
Can FOSA approach empirical capability frontiers? FOSA discovers tasks closer to the em-
pirical frontier on every robot except humanoid, where FOSA-RND scores marginally higher on
Frontier-Prox. The gap reflects the larger and sparser symbolic space of whole-body humanoid
control, in which an RND-style novelty bonus can occasionally reach frontier skeletons before the
learned generator has accumulated reward feedback.
Tasks that appear semantically easy can be physically hard, and vice versa. For hand, a simple
cube object is never reoriented even under loose orientation tolerance, whereas a ball of similar size
is reoriented in more than half of the tasks. For vehicle, halving the wheel torque or adding more
than 10 cm of terrain relief barely changes velocity tracking success, and for quadruped a 30 %
torque loss matters only above 0.5 m s−1. Such inversions are invisible to semantic plausibility and
should be identified by grounding task generation in physical policy learning.
Can FOSA generalize to unseen tasks? Fig. 5 reports the distilled policy on the discovered tasks
and Table 1 on the 16 held-out tasks. Averaged over held-out tasks, the policy distilled from FOSA-
discovered tasks attains the highest normalized reward (0.83), ahead of FOSA-LLM (0.74) and the
strongest baseline, MCTS (0.63), with the largest margins on quadruped and humanoid, where

Reward sensitivity. On the distinct skeletons trained by all methods except LLM-CL, we vary
one weight of V at a time between 1/4× and 4× its default and track the expected frontier score of
P ⋆(x) ∝R(x). Since F dominates V, λnov ∈[0, 1.6] and λcost ∈[0, 0.2] move the score by at
most 2 % and 0.2 %. Only β matters: halving it lowers the score by 15 % to 44 % and quartering it
by 46 % to 79 %, leaving 29 % to 65 % of the target’s mass on the frontier instead of 98 % to 100 %;
quadrupling it gains at most 16 % but drops over a third of the goal families on quadruped. The
default is thus the smallest β that concentrates on the frontier without losing coverage.
Budget sensitivity. Frontier tasks keep accumulating throughout discovery: after 64, 128, and 192
trained tasks, FOSA has found 22 % to 25 %, 48 % to 56 %, and 80 % to 92 % of its final frontier
tasks across robots. Frontier membership is also decided early in each task’s training: rescoring F
after 56 % of the PPO budget retains a rank correlation of 0.79 to 0.92 with the full-budget ranking
and keeps 85 % to 96 % of frontier labels, so the per-task budget could be cut by roughly 40 %
without changing what FOSA discovers.
Convergence. On every robot, the trajectory-balance loss falls by four orders of magnitude over 200
discovery rounds and the learned log Z flattens after about 128. On the pooled replayed skeletons,
the learned flow tracks the target reward (Spearman ρ = 0.90, Pearson r = 0.98, MAE 0.094 in
log R). These diagnostics verify the online objective empirically; the archive-dependent novelty
term precludes a stationary-reward guarantee.

5.3
Real-World Results
We test whether capabilities discovered in simulation transfer zero-shot to novel tasks on a ClearPath
Jackal and a Unitree Go2, each running on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin with an Intel RealSense D435i
at 30 Hz for policy inference and an OS1-64 LiDAR for 100 Hz odometry [60, 61]. Each robot has
four held-out tasks (Fig. 7): grass traversal, step climbing, obstacle bypass, and cart pushing for
vehicle; grass traversal, barrier balance, mug delivery, and tunnel crawl for quadruped.
Do discovered capabilities transfer to hardware? Table 2 reports success over six trials per
method–task pair. The trend matches simulation: policies distilled from FOSA-discovered tasks
transfer more reliably than those from baseline task sets. Absolute success is low for every method,
which we attribute to the lightweight student and the restricted simulation training protocol (Sec. 6).
The hardest task is barrier balance: narrow support, low-friction contact, and posture transitions
amplify errors the simulator does not fully reproduce. Within these constraints, FOSA achieves the
highest real-world success, supporting capability-frontier discovery as a practical source of transfer-
able robot training.
These results support FOSA’s central premise. Because the task space is combinatorial, discovery
must allocate its budget to tasks that are both learnable and informative, and the two failure modes
are asymmetric: a task already solved at initialization adds no behavioral coverage, whereas a task
still unsolved after training yields a teacher whose actions are uninformative or inconsistent. By
sampling in proportion to a reward that is positive only on the frontier, FOSA fills its archive with
tasks whose teachers encode nontrivial yet reliable control, the regime in which distillation is most
sample-efficient. The archive thereby contains transferable subskills (e.g., balance regulation, multi-
stage navigation) that the student recombines on tasks never seen during discovery, so the gains on
unseen tasks reflect better transfer of discovered capabilities as well as better discovery.
6
Conclusion and Limitations
We presented Generative Flow on Symbolic Abstractions (FOSA), a framework for discovering
embodiment-specific capabilities by learning an abstract policy over symbolic task skeletons. FOSA
compiles generated skeletons into simulation environments and uses low-level policy-learning per-
formance as feedback to discover tasks near the robot’s empirical capability frontier. Across four
heterogeneous robots, FOSA improves discovery efficiency, task diversity, and frontier proximity,
and the policy distilled from its tasks executes unseen instructions on real hardware.
Limitations. Empirical frontier. The frontier depends on the simulator, policy class, reward design,
and training budget, although its ranking is stable across budgets (Sec. 5.2). Sim-to-real. The student
is a lightweight language-conditioned transformer rather than a full VLA, and compute limits kept
domain randomization and task training below those of dedicated sim-to-real pipelines [4, 5]. Safety.
FOSA targets tasks at the edge of competence, so rollouts risk falls and collisions; all hardware runs
used an operator-held emergency stop and joint velocity limits, and automatic safety filtering of
generated tasks is left for future work.

success predicate, and termination conditions. The argument vector θx contains task-specific pa-

use slope, roughness, and commanded velocity, while manipulation tasks may use object size, object

Vehicle. Fig. A2.1 shows the real-world vehicle trajectories of all compared methods in the recon-
structed pointcloud map. The route consists of four stages: task C, obstacle bypass; task A, grass
traversal; task B, step climbing; and task D, cart pushing. FOSA is the only method that completes
the full route. The baseline methods usually fail before reaching task A, often due to obstacles or
scene configurations that are not well covered during capability discovery. This trend is consistent
with the main-paper results: baselines either discover less diverse task sets or fewer tasks near the
empirical capability frontier.
The final cart-pushing stage is especially challenging for the ablations because the cart introduces
nontrivial wheel dynamics and delayed contact effects. Although FOSA succeeds, the rollout also
exposes limitations of the distilled language-conditioned policy: the vehicle sometimes produces
jerky motions in the unstructured environment. Overall, these results suggest that FOSA benefits
from discovering diverse, frontier-proximal capabilities, while mobile robot policies still face chal-
lenges when interacting with complex real-world objects.
Quadruped. In the grass-traversal task, most methods struggle with uneven terrain and traction
variation, indicating that language-level task plausibility alone does not reliably capture the phys-
ical difficulty of the scene. In the barrier-balance task, no method fully completes the task, but
FOSA makes the most progress: the policy is able to maintain balance on the narrow barrier without
immediately falling off.
The mug-delivery task is intuitively simple, but it requires the robot to stabilize a carried object while
traversing uneven terrain. Only FOSA and its ablation FOSA-LLM keep the mug stable through the
traversal; the baselines lose it at the first terrain transition. In the tunnel crawl task, all methods can
pass through the tunnel, but only FOSA and its ablation lower the body height as intended. We also
observe that the FOSA policy can pass the tunnel using a reverse-motion strategy, suggesting that
the distilled policy can exploit non-obvious behaviors discovered during capability generation.

Distilling one student takes about 13 h to 19 h on four GPUs.
