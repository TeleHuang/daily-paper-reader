Abstract 

Evaluating robot manipulation policies through repeated physical trials is costly. We study whether an action-conditioned world model can serve as a closed-loop test environment whose policy success rates track those on real robots. Beyond realistic video, the model must return the camera views and proprioceptive state needed for the policy’s next action and preserve failed-action outcomes to avoid overestimating policy capability. We introduce ProWorld, a world model that jointly predicts multi-view video and robot state, then feeds both back to a frozen policy for repeated interaction. ProWorld comprises a shared transformer for joint video–state flow prediction and Shared-Time View Encoding to distinguish cameras while preserving temporal synchronization. To reduce interaction hallucinations that turn missed grasps into successful lifts, we augment training with failed interactions collected in high-fidelity simulation, reducing inflated success rates in the generated environment. ProWorld achieves the highest overall TriWorldBench score among the compared methods. Across three policies and four local manipulation tasks, it attains a Pearson correlation of 0.95 and a Spearman correlation of 0.94 between generated and real success rates. These results support joint visual-proprioceptive feedback and failure-aware training as a basis for world-model-based policy evaluation. 1arXiv:2610.15294v1 [cs.RO] 11 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Evaluating robot policies through physical trials is costly, both in terms of robot time and the need for repeated scene resets. With the development of action-conditioned video foundation models (Yang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026), recent work has extended these models to policy evaluation (Guo et al., 2025; GigaWorld Team et al., 2026; Jeon et al., 2026; Tseng et al., 2025). These approaches use learned world models as closed-loop evaluation environments: initialized from a real observation, the world model predicts the consequences of actions executed by a fixed policy and generates the observations used for subsequent policy decisions. The goal is to estimate policy success rates through generated closed-loop interactions that faithfully reflect real-robot performance. However, two requirements that are largely overlooked by existing approaches remain critical for reliable policy evaluation. First, the world model should provide the complete observation space required by the policy, including multiple camera views and proprioceptive states such as joint positions. Second, it must faithfully preserve unsuccessful interactions. For example, if a missed grasp is subsequently rendered as a successful lift, the world model produces an interaction hallucination : it predicts a successful outcome that is not supported by the executed action. Such hallucinations can inflate estimated success rates and obscure genuine policy failures. A reliable evaluator therefore requires both complete visual–proprioceptive feedback and faithful modeling of failure outcomes. We introduce ProWorld, which jointly predicts multi-view videos and robot state from observation history and an action chunk, and then feeds both back to a frozen policy. A shared transformer learns both outputs through joint flow matching. State queries read the visual stream through asymmetric attention, encouraging agreement between predicted state and visible motion. The policy thus receives predicted execution feedback rather than its preceding command as the next state. To preserve camera identity and synchronization, we develop Shared-Time View Encoding (STVE). Independently encoded camera streams receive shared time coordinates and explicit view coordinates. This avoids pixel-tiling boundaries and separates camera identity from elapsed time. Compatible with pretrained video backbones, STVE improves visual prediction over pixel tiling and time concatenation methods. To reduce interaction hallucinations, we augment demonstrations and robot-policy rollouts with failed interactions collected in high-fidelity simulation. Successful demonstrations provide limited evidence of what happens when actions fail. For example, a missed grasp may leave the object in place, while an unintended push may knock it over. Simulated failures supply this missing supervision. In our local evaluation, this training reduces hallucinated successes and preserves failed interactions. We assess policy-outcome agreement and video prediction quality. Across three policies and four local tasks, ProWorld achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.95 and a Spearman correlation of 0.94 with real success rates. We also evaluate two policies on six RoboChallenge tasks. On TriWorldBench, ProWorld scores 53.68, exceeding the strongest compared baseline’s 51.72. Our contributions are threefold: (1) Joint video–state prediction that supplies visual observations and learned proprioceptive feedback for closed-loop policy interaction; (2) Shared-Time View Encoding ,which preserves camera identity and synchronization while improving visual prediction over pixel tiling and time concatenation; and (3) Failure-aware training that uses simulated failures to reduce interaction hallucinations and success-rate inflation.

Multi-view, multimodal, and world–action models. Multiple cameras expose complementary evidence about contact and motion. Multi-View Masked World Models learns visual representations from multiple viewpoints (Seo et al., 2023), while recent generative systems study joint multi-view prediction and cross-embodiment synthesis (Guo et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026). World–action models (WAMs) use predictive world representations to produce executable actions: DreamZero jointly models future video and actions, and Motus unifies world modeling, control, and inverse dynamics (Ye et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2025). Attention structure. Video and state share a backbone but have different visibility. In bidirectional training, context and video tokens attend to each other; state queries read video and state tokens. Video queries do not read the noisy state stream. This makes state prediction a visual readout while retaining joint supervision of the backbone. For autoregressive training, each noisy chunk reads clean preceding chunks and its own noisy tokens, but not the clean target for that chunk or any future chunk. The state stream also reads preceding states. Figure 3 summarizes these masks. During rollout, denoising reads a fixed history cache; the completed chunk refreshes the cache for the next interaction. 

3.3 Shared-Time View Encoding 

Figure 2 compares three ways to organize multi-view inputs. Pixel tiling , used by Cosmos 3 and GigaWorld-1 (NVIDIA et al., 2026; GigaWorld Team et al., 2026), combines camera images before VAE encoding, reusing the video pipeline but introducing artificial spatial boundaries. Time concatenation , used in 5Cosmos-Predict2.5’s multi-view implementation (NVIDIA et al., 2025) and ReCamMaster’s source–target conditioning (Bai et al., 2025), independently encodes each camera stream and concatenates the resulting tokens along the temporal sequence dimension. 

STVE instead assigns camera identity its own rotary coordinate (Figure 2, right). Each camera is encoded independently, with tokens indexed by (t, h, w, v ): synchronized views share time t, (h, w ) remain local to each camera, and v identifies the view. Disjoint rotary subspaces encode these coordinates: 

R(t, h, w, v ) = diag( Rt(t), R h(h), R w(w), R v(v)) , eqi = Riqi, eki = Riki. (5) Joint attention thus distinguishes views without spatial seams or view-dependent time offsets, using the same coordinates for history and generated chunks. The view coordinate fits within the existing attention-head width: MiniMax-H3 uses its unrotated tail, while Wan reserves a small portion of its spatial rotary channels, preserving the temporal allocation. STVE adds neither tokens nor a separate cross-view attention module. Table 5 compares the three structures in our model.

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Training data and implementation. We pretrain on approximately 6,486 hours of heterogeneous manipula-tion data, combining large open datasets with a smaller private collection. The mixture includes InternData-A1 (264 h) (Tian et al., 2025), RoboCOIN (312 h) (Wu et al., 2025b), ABC-130K (2,420 h) (Allshire et al., 2026), InternData-M1 (156 h) (Chen et al., 2025b), AgiBotWorld-Beta (2,233 h) (AgiBot-World-Contributors et al., 2025), RealOmni-Open (199 h) (Genrobot, 2026), Galaxea Open-World (119 h) (Jiang et al., 2025), and RoboMIND (60 h) (Wu et al., 2025a), together with 71 hours of GenieSim data containing diverse failure trajectories and 652 hours of private data. We use MiniMax-H3 (MiniMax, 2026) and Wan (Team Wan et al., 2025) as video diffusion backbones, with details on their use and training settings provided in Appendix A.1. 

Evaluation settings. The local closed-loop study uses three policies on four manipulation tasks, with Ctrl-World and Cosmos 3 Nano as alternative rollout environments. A second study evaluates two policies on six challenging RoboChallenge tasks (Yakefu et al., 2025), covering bimanual handling, placement, sweeping, stacking, and knot tying (Figure 5). For the representation ablations, we initialize models from a pretrained backbone and train them on AgiBotWorld-Alpha (AgiBot-World-Contributors et al., 2025). ProWorld’s generated success rates closely track real outcomes across 12 local policy–task pairs (Figure 6), with Pearson r = 0 .95 and Spearman ρ = 0 .94 . Both correlations exceed those of Ctrl-World ( 0.33 /0.26 )and Cosmos 3 Nano ( 0.67 /0.54 ), indicating stronger agreement in success rates and outcome ordering. Failure data improves Pearson/Spearman correlation from 0.93 /0.91 to 0.95 /0.94 . The fitted relation changes from y = 0 .76 x + 18 .41 to y = 0 .82 x + 10 .53 , reducing overestimation at low real success rates. This supports transferring failure supervision across embodiments. Head and wrist observations provide complementary constraints on robot motion and scene interaction. On the single-view WorldArena benchmark, ProWorld performs especially well on trajectory accuracy, photometric consistency, and semantic alignment (Table 3). Together, these results indicate that motion, persistent scene appearance, and task content survive the generated transition. 4.4 Ablations 

Further ablations of action conditioning, visual context, noise schedules, and training resolution are provided in Appendix B. 

Action representation and joint state learning. In Table 4, velocity-style control improves visual predic-tion over absolute-state conditioning by directly relating the commanded change to visible motion. Adding state supervision improves the shared visual representation and supplies proprioceptive feedback. Motion-based conditioning with asymmetric attention gives the strongest combination: state queries read the evolving video, while state loss trains the backbone to retain robot-motion information. Compared with full attention between noisy streams, asymmetric attention lowers VSC and improves motion direction with little change in gripper accuracy, thereby strengthening agreement between predicted continuous motion and the generated video. 

Multi-view encoding. Table 5 compares the three structures in Figure 2. Time concatenation improves all video metrics over pixel tiling. Replacing its view-dependent temporal offsets with STVE further raises PSNR 10 from 21.56 to 22.60 and SSIM from 0.809 to 0.829, and lowers LPIPS from 0.1266 to 0.1069. State results are mixed: STVE improves directional agreement and matches gripper accuracy, but time concatenation has lower VSC. # 5 Conclusion and Limitations 

ProWorld evaluates robot policies through closed-loop interaction, combining joint visual–proprioceptive feedback, STVE for camera identity and synchronization, and simulated failures to reduce interaction hallucinations and success-rate inflation. Strong correlation with real policy success and competitive video prediction support complete observation feedback and faithful failure outcomes as foundations for policy evaluation. Object deformation and drift over exceptionally long rollouts remain limitations. Improving geometry persistence and rollout stability could reduce accumulated evaluation errors.

A.1 Training Details 

Backbones and initial fine-tuning. We instantiate ProWorld with two pretrained video backbones: Wan2.1-14B and MiniMax-H3. We perform full-parameter fine-tuning on Wan2.1-14B and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on MiniMax-H3, using learning rates of 2 × 10 −5 and 10 −4, respectively. Both models are initially fine-tuned on our proposed dataset for 36,000 steps with a batch size of 64. 

Wan-based training and experiments. Following this initial fine-tuning, we continue training the Wan-based model using the standard Helios Stage 1 procedure with an autoregressive attention mask. The ablation studies in the main text and the RoboChallenge experiments use this Wan-based model. 

MiniMax-H3-based training and experiments. For MiniMax-H3, we follow the standard minWM pipeline for long-video generation training and distillation after the initial fine-tuning. The additional ablation studies in Appendix B use the MiniMax-H3-based model. 

A.2 Action/State Definitions and Temporal Alignment 

Definitions and layouts. The control feature uk conditions prediction, whereas sk is the absolute propri-oceptive target. End-effector data use 32-dimensional vectors: each arm has Cartesian position (3), a 6D rotation representation (6), and gripper opening (1), occupying entries 0–9 and 10–19; unused entries are zero. Continuous pose coordinates are median-filtered and Savitzky–Golay smoothed before differencing at the control rate. Translation features have units of length per second; rotation features are 6D-coordinate rates, not angular velocities. Grippers retain raw absolute openings. State targets remain the original absolute poses and openings. These trajectory-derived controls are motion proxies. For joint data with command logs, absolute commanded and measured positions remain separate. The RoboTwin adapter, for example, places each arm’s six joints and gripper in entries 0–6 and 16–22, with zeros elsewhere. 

Normalization. Nu and Ns follow the dataset/embodiment schema. Scalar coordinates use 1st/99th-percentile affine scaling; absolute 6D rotations retain their bounded representation. Velocity features use separate statistics, with continuous coordinates clipped to [−5, 5] and grippers to [−1, 1] after normalization. Joint data use joint-layout statistics. State predictions are mapped back to native units before policy feedback; clipping is not invertible. 

Temporal alignment. Synchronized views share source-frame indices. For clip start b, source rate fs,video rate fv, and control rate fu, the binary loader samples 

ivj = b + round( jf s/f v), iuk = b + round( kf s/f u). (6) The initial image uses j = 0 ; target controls start at k = 1 . Each state target is read at its control’s source index. At equal video/control rates, (uk, s k) aligns with target image Ok. For k > 1, the derived control describes the interval ending at that sample. The first sampled velocity of each continuous coordinate is zero; the gripper retains its absolute value. Across rates, alignment follows physical time rather than equal array positions. Fields and serialization. The metadata prompt describes the data source, embodiment, and observation setup in a compact JSON object. Its fields have a fixed order: dataset identifies the dataset group or individual data subset; robot names the embodiment; fps gives the control sampling rate in Hz; deploy 

specifies the domain (e.g., real or simulation ); and views lists camera names in the same order as the input video streams. Thus, fps corresponds to fu in Appendix A.2, and the camera list is reordered whenever the views are shuffled. 

Example and optional task text. For AgiBotWorld-Alpha at 10 Hz, group-level metadata take the following form (line breaks are added for readability): 

> {"dataset":"agibot/agibot_alpha","robot":"agibot_alpha", "fps":10,"deploy":"real", "views":["head_color","hand_left_color","hand_right_color"]}

The loader serializes this object without extra whitespace. Group-level naming permits samples with the same metadata to share a prompt; subset-level naming retains the individual dataset identifier while removing a trailing train/evaluation split name. If task conditioning is enabled and a nonempty instruction is available, a final task string is appended to the object. Otherwise, that field is omitted. Numeric actions and proprioceptive targets are supplied through the separate interface in Appendix A.2, rather than inserted into this JSON object. 

A.4 Failure Data Collection 

Perturbed physical replay. We generate missed-grasp examples from successful GenieSim trajectories. Each replay restores the recorded scene and initial robot–object poses, then tracks the recorded joint trajectory with a Cartesian offset applied to the active arm. A damped least-squares Jacobian update maps the offset to joint corrections, ramped in before gripper closure and maintained during lifting. The target object remains a dynamic rigid body, allowing contact and gravity to determine the outcome; non-target objects remain kinematic. 

Failure selection and recording. We label a perturbed replay as a missed grasp only when the target’s maximum vertical displacement from its first replay frame is below a threshold adjusted during collection and bounded by 5 cm. Replays reaching this threshold are excluded from the failure set. Each replay saves synchronized camera videos, the perturbed joint reference trajectory, and per-frame target and end-effector poses. A manifest links each variant to its successful source and records the requested offset, achieved offset at closure, and lift-based label. 

A.5 Closed-Loop Evaluation Configuration 

Adaptation and policy prerequisites. Before closed-loop evaluation, we fine-tune ProWorld for the target embodiment and tasks using trajectories collected on the corresponding real-robot platform. GenieSim failure trajectories constitute 10% of the fine-tuning mixture. Evaluation additionally requires locally deployable robot-policy weights and real-robot success rates for the same policies and tasks. 

Closed-loop protocol. Evaluation recordings provide only the initial frames for world-model rollouts; subsequent recorded video and actions are never supplied to either ProWorld or the policy. After initialization, the policy acts on observations generated by ProWorld, which predicts the next multi-view observations and 16 proprioceptive state for continued policy feedback. Thus, all subsequent actions are chosen online by the policy. Below, one interaction round denotes one policy query followed by the corresponding world-model transition. 

RoboChallenge. We use the Table30v2 dataset for adaptation and evaluate π0.5 and DM0.5, selected for their publicly released fine-tuned policy weights and nontrivial success rates reported on the official test platform. We use these official rates as the real-robot reference and collect 120 evaluation trajectories from the RoboChallenge website across the two policies and six tasks. These recordings supply initial frames under the protocol above. Most tasks require more than 80 interaction rounds to complete; success in the world-model rollouts is judged manually. 

Cobot. For our local Cobot evaluation, we collect 200 real-robot trajectories across three policies and four tasks. These recordings serve only to measure real-robot policy success rates and provide initial frames for world-model evaluation. Cobot tasks require approximately 25 policy–world-model interaction rounds. A locally deployed vision–language model (VLM) scores the generated rollouts to determine task success.

B.1 Trajectory-Quality Evaluation Protocol 

Inputs and alignment. The AgiBotWorld-Alpha ablations assess video–state agreement and state prediction separately. For video–state agreement, a fixed inverse dynamics model (IDM) reads only the head-camera video at 10 FPS, resized to 224 × 224 . We remove the initial conditioning frame to align video frames with saved state predictions. The IDM takes pairs of frames 16 steps apart and predicts 16 motion steps; overlapping predictions are averaged and denormalized. Cumulative motion is anchored at the first state of the trajectory being compared, while gripper outputs remain absolute. 

Video–state consistency (VSC). Let E(V, S ) denote the mean Euclidean distance, in millimeters, between the IDM-reconstructed end-effector positions from video V and the positions in state sequence S. We average over time and both arms within each clip, then equally over clips. With generated video ˆV , predicted states ˆS,and recorded references (V, S ), we report 

VSC = E( ˆV , ˆS) − E(V, S ). (7) Thus, VSC measures excess video–state disagreement relative to the IDM’s reference-video error; lower is better. This reference error is an empirical calibration, not a guaranteed lower bound. VSC covers the head view only and does not measure task success. 

State prediction (Dir. and Grip.). Dir. is the motion-direction cosine similarity between predicted and ground-truth end-effector trajectories. Grip. is the accuracy of predicted gripper open/closed states against ground truth. Both are higher-is-better measures computed from predicted versus reference states, indepen-dently of the IDM. B.3 State-Loss Weight 

The state-loss weight controls how strongly proprioceptive supervision contributes to joint video–state learning. Figure 9 varies λs, the coefficient of the state term in the main text’s joint flow-matching objective, over {0, 0.05 , 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 15 , 100 }. The figure uses λ as shorthand for λs; λs = 0 removes state supervision, and state-related metrics are omitted for this setting. A moderate state-loss weight provides a favorable balance between visual quality and video–state agreement. Among the tested values, λs = 2 yields the lowest LPIPS and VSC and the highest gripper accuracy, while PSNR and SSIM are slightly higher at λs = 1 . Motion-direction similarity does not peak at 

λs = 2 , so this choice does not optimize every metric individually. Larger weights, particularly 15 and 100, degrade visual quality and increase VSC relative to 2. We therefore adopt λs = 2 for all experiments with state outputs, except for this explicit weight sweep.
