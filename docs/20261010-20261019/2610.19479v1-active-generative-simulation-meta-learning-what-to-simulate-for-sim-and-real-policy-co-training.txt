Abstract: Sim-and-real co-training is an effective way to scale robot learning
by augmenting limited real-world demonstrations with simulation data. How-
ever, existing approaches typically treat simulators as fixed data sources, re-
lying on uniform generation, offline retrieval, or manual parameter alignment
by domain experts. We formulate Active Generative Simulation: optimizing a
simulator’s data-generation distribution to maximize downstream real-world per-
formance. To solve this problem, we propose MetaGenSim, a meta-learning
framework that trains an auxiliary rater network via meta-gradients to automat-
ically discover high-utility data-generation distributions within a user-specified
simulation parameter space, without manual parameter alignment. Across sim-
ulation and real-world manipulation tasks, MetaGenSim outperforms uniform
generation and retrieval baselines, achieves a 150% relative improvement in
real-world full-task success over uniform generation, and matches expert man-
ual alignment.
We further show that distributions curated with a lightweight
proxy policy transfer to VLA fine-tuning, and that MetaGenSim can exploit
partial alignment through atomic skill stitching and object-receptacle composi-
tion when exact sim-and-real task alignment is unavailable.

Introduction
While recent large-scale real-robot datasets have advanced generalist policies [1, 2, 3], relying ex-
clusively on real data remains expensive, labor-intensive, and safety-constrained. Generative sim-
ulation offers a scalable alternative data source by procedurally synthesizing scene layouts, assets,
arXiv:2610.19479v1  [cs.RO]  14 Oct 2026


and tasks [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. We refer to the variables controlling these environments, such as camera
poses, object arrangements, visual appearance, and task goals, as data generation parameters.
Given data generation parameters, a generative simulator can instantiate environments and generate
large numbers of demonstrations with minimal human intervention [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. However,
the utility of simulation data remains limited by the sim-to-real gap. Domain randomization and
system identification can mitigate this mismatch, but often require substantial human effort and
cannot eliminate residual gaps [14]. A promising alternative is sim-and-real co-training, where
real demonstrations anchor learning to the deployment domain while simulation provides scale and
diversity [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Co-training can outperform training on real data alone [17, 16, 20], but
not all generated data is useful: misaligned camera poses, object layouts, visual factors, or task goals
can provide little benefit, and exact alignment may be impossible when the simulator lacks the target
setting. This raises a central question: Among the vast space of possible simulation environments,
which data should be generated to maximize the performance of a sim-and-real co-trained policy?
Current simulation data-generation strategies largely rely on manual heuristics [21, 22] or uniform
randomization [23, 24]. Overly broad training distributions can degrade policy learning when many
samples are irrelevant to the target task [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Prior work suggests that task-aware
alignment helps [16], but manually tuning high-dimensional generation parameters is laborious,
requires expert knowledge, and can be misleading when exact alignment is impossible.
In this work, we formulate Active Generative Simulation: determining a distribution over simulation
parameters such that co-training on the resulting synthetic data maximizes target-task performance.
To efficiently tackle this problem, we propose MetaGenSim, a meta-learning framework that uses
a sparse probing dataset to train an auxiliary rater network, which assigns utility scores to simulation
samples based on their contribution to real-world validation performance, automatically guiding the
active generation of a targeted synthetic dataset. In summary, our core contributions are:
• We formulate Active Generative Simulation, shifting simulation data generation from fixed distri-
bution to controllable and targeted synthesis.
• We introduce MetaGenSim, a meta-learning framework that trains an auxiliary rater network
via meta-gradients to discover high-utility data-generation distributions within a user-specified
parameter space, without manual parameter alignment.
• We show that MetaGenSim outperforms uniform generation and offline retrieval, achieving a
150% relative improvement in real-world full-task success over uniform generation while match-
ing the performance of manual expert alignment.
• We demonstrate scalability and generalization of MetaGenSim: proxy-curated datasets transfer
to fine-tuning π0.5, and MetaGenSim can exploit partially aligned data when exact task align-
ment is unavailable.

Related Works
Generative simulation. Recent progress in generative modeling [30, 31], motion synthesis [9, 32,
33], and procedural environment generation [4, 5] has enabled automated simulation pipelines that
scale synthetic robot data [34, 7, 35, 23, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. These systems can generate assets, tasks,
scenes, and demonstrations with limited human intervention. However, most synthesize feasible data
passively without optimizing which specific samples best improve a downstream policy. In contrast,
MetaGenSim leverages a small set of target-task data to actively guide simulation data generation
toward high-utility parameter regions.
Sim-and-real alignment and policy co-training. To leverage simulation data for real-robot de-
ployment, policies must overcome the sim-to-real gap [24, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 16, 23, 17, 18].
Domain randomization perturbs visual and physical properties to encourage robustness [24, 41, 42],
while system identification tunes simulator parameters to match real-world dynamics or appear-
ance [46, 43, 45]. These approaches assume there exist high-fidelity alignments, but tighter match-
ing does not necessarily translate to better policy learning when residual gaps remain [44]. Recent
2


sim-and-real co-training methods instead mix simulated and real demonstrations, showing strong
empirical gains [16, 23, 17, 18, 19]. MetaGenSim builds on this co-training paradigm but treats
the generative simulator as a controllable distribution, actively optimizing data synthesis rather than
manually tuning the simulator to match the real world.
Data attribution and retrieval in robotics. Recent work shows that heterogeneous robot datasets
can cause negative transfer on specific tasks [26, 28, 25, 29, 47, 48, 49]. Retrieval and attribution
methods address this by selecting useful samples from existing datasets using embedding similar-
ity [29, 50, 47, 49], rollout outcomes [26], or validation loss [25]. However, these methods are
fundamentally limited by the coverage of the static prior dataset and cannot synthesize new target-
relevant data. MetaGenSim is complementary: it uses utility estimation to generate new simulation
data tailored to the target task.

The Active Generative Simulation Problem
We formalize Active Generative Simulation as the problem of optimizing a simulator’s data gener-
ation distribution to maximize downstream policy performance on a real-world target task. Rather
than treating the simulator as a fixed data source, we formulate it as a controllable generator param-
eterized by spatial, visual, and task-level variables, such as object placements, camera poses, and
textures. By treating data generation itself as an optimization problem, our goal is to actively allocate
the simulation budget toward the parameter regions most valuable for sim-and-real co-training.
3.1
Generative Simulation and Co-Training
We denote the generative simulator as G : Φ →Ξ, where Φ is the space of data generation parame-
ters and Ξ is the space of expert trajectories. Given ϕ ∈Φ, the simulator generates a demonstration
ξ ∼G(ϕ). The parameter vector ϕ controls variables such as task identity, object placements, cam-
era configuration, and visual appearance. In our real-world experiments, Φ includes task mode, table
texture (3D), camera yaw (1D), and object placements (4D). We assume that Φ, its ranges, and the
underlying generation pipeline are specified by the user; our goal is to optimize the data-generation
distribution within this space rather than to design the space itself.

Experiments
In this section, we design experiments across simulation and real-world manipulation tasks to eval-
uate MetaGenSim. Specifically, our evaluations answer four questions: RQ1 (Efficacy): Does
MetaGenSim improve sim-and-real co-training over real-only training, uniform generation, re-
trieval, and manual alignment? RQ2 (Mechanism): Does MetaGenSim learn target-aware utility
scores and data-generation distributions? RQ3 (Cross-architecture transfer): Do distributions cu-
rated with a lightweight proxy policy transfer to large VLA policies? RQ4 (Partial alignment):
When exact sim-and-real alignment is impossible, can MetaGenSim still exploit partially aligned
simulation data to improve target performance?

stackCube, mugBin, bowlTray, and mugHang. We collect only 10 real demonstrations for each task.
In simulation, the source task set contains the same four tasks, and the data generation parameters
include a discrete task mode and 8 continuous dimensions: camera yaw (1D), table RGB (3D), and
object placements (4D). We instantiate the generative simulator with customized MimicLabs [54]

Data quality matters more than na¨ıvely increasing the number of demonstrations. To test
whether the gains come simply from adding more simulation data, we scale UniformGen by 5× on
stackCube and mugBin. As shown in Appendix Table 4, increasing uniformly generated demonstra-

targeted data generation is more important than data quantity alone.

approach for curating useful data from a fixed prior dataset, and both STRAP and IWR improve
over UniformGen on several real-world tasks. However, retrieval is limited by the coverage of the

achieves substantially higher real-world co-training performance than both retrieval baselines.

5.4
Handling Irreducible Sim-and-Real Misalignment (RQ4)
The previous sections show that MetaGenSim can align simulation parameters to real-world tasks
despite visual rendering gaps; here, we study a stronger form of misalignment where the exact target
task cannot be generated directly in the simulator. We test whether MetaGenSim can synthesize
data mixtures that support two forms of generalization: atomic skill stitching for long-horizon be-
haviors and object-receptacle composition for novel real tasks.
Atomic skill stitching for long-horizon behaviors. We first examine long-horizon tasks whose
exact unified behaviors are absent from the source task set. For openDrawerPutBowl and putBowl-
CloseDrawer, full task-level alignment is impossible; useful co-training data must instead come
from related atomic tasks. As shown in Figure 3, MetaGenSim remains effective by using meta-
gradient feedback to prioritize necessary source-task components. On putBowlCloseDrawer, for ex-
ample, MetaGenSim even outperforms Oracle by generating a non-obvious mixture of binBowl,
putBowlInDrawer, and closeDrawer. This demonstrates that MetaGenSim can curate targeted data
that helps policies stitch reusable simulation skills over time under irreducible task misalignment.

lated skills, generating most of its co-training data
from mugBin and bowlTray, and achieves higher
performance than UniformGen. These results
show that MetaGenSim can synthesize reusable
simulation-skill mixtures that enable policies to
compose learned object and receptacle affor-
dances for novel real-world target setups.
6
Conclusion
We introduced MetaGenSim, a meta-learning framework for Active Generative Simulation that
optimizes what to simulate for sim-and-real co-training. MetaGenSim learns a target-aware rater
from meta-gradients, converts rater scores into data-generation distributions, and synthesizes tar-
geted simulation datasets for improved sim-and-real co-training. Across simulation and real-world
manipulation tasks, MetaGenSim improves over real-only training, uniform generation, and offline
retrieval, while matching the performance of expert-aligned simulation data. We further show that
8


proxy-curated datasets transfer to VLA fine-tuning, and that MetaGenSim can exploit partially
aligned simulation data through atomic skill stitching and object-receptacle composition when exact
sim-and-real alignment is unavailable.
7
Limitations
User-specified parameter space and generation pipeline. MetaGenSim optimizes the data-
generation distribution within a user-specified parameter space Φ, including its ranges and the un-
derlying generation pipeline (e.g., MimicGen source demonstrations). It can therefore only exploit
factors exposed in Φ and data the pipeline can produce. Unexposed factors, such as background and
lighting in our real-world setup, remain unaligned. This parameterization is straightforward in our
domains but may not scale to broader simulators that represent tasks, visuals, and configurations
differently. Developing a unified parameter space, potentially through learned latent parameters that
make the generation pipeline more differentiable, is an exciting future direction.
Probing coverage and density estimation. The rater can only score regions covered by the probing
dataset, which we sample uniformly in a single round. Sparse probing may miss small high-utility
regions, and weighted KDE becomes less reliable as the dimension of Φ grows. Adaptive or iterative
probing that refines the distribution over multiple rounds is a natural extension.
Distribution concentration and hyperparameters. We impose no explicit diversity or coverage
constraint. Instead, the spread of the generated distribution is controlled by the temperatures and
the KDE bandwidth. Overly small bandwidths or overly high temperatures degrade performance
(Appendix Table 6), and a narrow target dataset can yield a narrow generated distribution.

B
Sim-and-Real Experiments Setup
Target Tasks. To validate the effectiveness of our method under realistic scenarios, we evaluate
MetaGenSim and baselines on four challenging real-world target tasks: stackCube, mugBin, bowl-
Tray, and mugHang. A visualization of 4 target tasks can be found in Figure 7. These tasks require
high-precision manipulation and object alignment. To enable fine-grained performance analysis, we
decouple the evaluation into two stages: Pick and Place. We report the success counts for Pick
and Pick+Place in Table 1. Detailed task descriptions and their respective success criteria are listed
below:
• stackCube: The robot must pick up a yellow cube and stack it on top of a green cube.
– Pick Success: The robot successfully grasps and lifts the yellow cube.
– Place Success: The yellow cube remains stably stacked on the green cube after the
robot releases its gripper.
• mugBin: The robot must pick up a mug and place it inside a bin.
– Pick Success: The mug is successfully grasped and lifted.
– Place Success: The mug remains inside the bin after the robot releases the gripper.
• bowlTray: The robot must pick up a bowl and place it onto a tray.
– Pick Success: The bowl is successfully grasped and lifted.
– Place Success: The bowl is placed flatly inside the tray after the gripper is released.
• mugHang: The robot must pick up a mug and hang it on a mug tree branch by its handle.
– Pick Success: The mug is successfully grasped and lifted.
– Place Success: The mug remains hanging on the mug tree branch after the robot
releases the gripper.
Target Dataset: We collected 10 demonstrations for each target task via teleoperation using a Meta
Quest 3 headset and controller. These real-world demonstrations form the target dataset, denoted as
DR.

Section 5, we parameterize the generative simula-
tor using Task (Discrete), Camera Pose (Contin-

very different from the real environment.
Although the parameter space Φ is continuous,
the generative simulator imposes validity con-
straints. For instance, object and receptacle posi-
tions cannot overlap to avoid collisions. Further-
more, the underlying demonstration generator,
MimicGen [9], requires distinct source demon-
strations for different spatial arrangements (e.g.,
swapping objects from left to right requires separate source demos). To satisfy these constraints
when generating Dprobe and the UniformGen dataset, we sample parameters from pre-defined
disjoint regions. Specifically, object positions are drawn from two distinct configuration settings:
(Object 1 on the left, Object 2 on the right) and (Object 1 on the right, Object 2 on the left), where
15


“left” and “right” denote specific 15cm × 20cm rectangular zones. Additionally, camera yaw is
sampled from 5 disjoint angular bins, and table textures are sampled from regions near black, white,
blue, and red. When using MetaGenSim to actively generate data, we do not restrict sampling to
these disjoint regions. Instead, we sample continuous values directly from the Gaussian kernels. For
object positions, we apply a simple rejection sampling step to prevent objects from being too close
to each other or out of the workspace.
Baselines and Datasets. We generate simulation demonstrations via MimicGen [9]. We compare
MetaGenSim against the following baselines, controlling for data budgets where applicable:
• MetaGenSim (Ours): We first generate a probing dataset Dprobe of 800 demos (Visual-
ization in Figure 8), which is used alongside DR to train the rater network (details in Ap-
pendix D). We then set a generation budget of 800 demos for the active generation phase.
Thus, the final MetaGenSim dataset consists of 800 demos sampled from the learned
distribution.
• Oracle: This dataset contains 800 demos generated with expert-aligned parameters: the
correct task, camera extrinsics from the real-world calibration, manually tuned object reset
ranges, and manually selected table colors.
• UniformGen: To ensure a fair comparison of total simulation samples, UniformGen
has access to the 800 probing demos plus an additional 800 uniformly generated demos,
totaling 1600 demos.
• STRAP and IWR: These retrieval baselines select a subset of data from the 1600-demo
UniformGen pool. Further implementation details for retrieval-based methods are pro-
vided in Appendix F.
We use the same source demos in MimicGen to generate all above simulation datasets.
Generation Hyperparameters. For all real-world tasks, we use the same default hyperparameters
when constructing the generation distribution: task temperature 0.5, trajectory-score temperature
0.05, and KDE multiplicative coefficients 0.6 for camera yaw, 0.2 for table RGB, and 0.8 for object
positions. The sensitivity of performance to these hyperparameters is analyzed in Appendix H.
After generating the simulation datasets, we co-train Diffusion Policy [55, 56] on a mixture of the
generated simulation data and the real-world data DR. We utilize a co-training ratio of α = 0.9,
which is determined by empirical findings in [17]. For details on training and evaluation procedures,
please refer to Appendix E.
For the real-world stackCube setting with up-and-down object placements (shown in Figure 11),
we treat the task as fixed rather than a data generation parameter. Consequently, for this specific
experiment, the probing dataset consists exclusively of samples from the stackCube task.

Sim-and-Sim Experiments Setup
We conduct controlled experiments in simulation to evaluate the efficacy of our meta-learning algo-
rithm in identifying simulation parameters that result in environments aligned with the target task.
These experiments are based on a subset of the MimicLabs dataset [54].
Domain Gap Construction. To simulate the sim-to-real gap, we manually create domain shifts
using the distinct “lab” environments provided in MimicLabs. The dataset contains 8 different labs,
each featuring unique background scenes and table textures. For each target task, we designate one
lab as the target domain (from which the few-shot target data is drawn) and the remaining 7 labs as
the source domains. The generative simulator is constrained to generate data only within the source
domains. Consequently, a domain gap is strictly enforced, as the visual appearance (background and
texture) of the generated data always differs from the target task.

(Left, Right, Center), and receptacle positions into 2 bins (Left, Right). Combined with the atomic
tasks and labs, this results in approximately 800 distinct parameter bins for the source data. For

The primary objective of the sim-and-sim experiments is to verify the Rater Network’s ability to ef-
fectively score aligned data (Section 4.1). Therefore, we apply a simplified version of MetaGenSim
for these specific experiments. Instead of constructing a continuous probability density function (as
in Section 4.2), we leverage the pre-existing bins described above. We rank the parameter bins
according to the CVaR scores of the probing samples and generate data from the top 2% of high-
scoring bins. The Dprobe and UniformGen datasets are generated uniformly across all bins, while
STRAP and IWR retrieve data from the UniformGen dataset.
It is important to emphasize that this bin-ranking approach is used only for the sim-and-sim setting
to validate the scoring mechanism. The Sim-and-Real experiments (detailed in Appendix B) utilize
the complete version of MetaGenSim. In the real-world setting, we do not group parameters into
bins; instead, we perform continuous density estimation (KDE) over the individual data generation
parameters to construct and sample from a smooth utility landscape.
After the source datasets are generated using different methods, we co-train BC-RNN and Diffusion
Policy (DP) [55, 56] on the mixture of the generated/retrieved source dataset and the few-shot target
dataset.
D
Details on MetaGenSim
Rater Network Architecture. The Rater network is designed to assign a scalar score to observation-
action pairs. It consists of an encoder followed by a MLP head. The encoder utilizes a ResNet18
backbone, identical to the one used in the policy architecture (see Appendix E). However, unlike the
policy, the Rater Network receives both observations and actions as inputs. Specifically, the action
vector is concatenated with the low-dimensional observation vector before being processed. The
combined features are then passed through an MLP with two hidden layers of size (256, 256) to
output the final scalar score.
Meta-Learning Implementation. The meta-learning procedure is detailed in Algorithm 1. Fol-
lowing [52], we employ the Adam optimizer [58] for both the inner (policy update) and outer (rater
update) loops. We utilize the differentiable Adam implementation from TorchOpt [59] to enable
higher-order meta-gradient backpropagation. We set the inner learning rate to 1e−4 (for the policy)
and the outer learning rate to 1e −5 (for the rater). For real-world experiments, we use T = 5 inner
gradient steps and a batch size of 64 for both the training and validation batches; this configuration
fits in a single NVIDIA A40 GPU. For simulation experiments, we found that T = 2 inner steps is
sufficient to learn an effective rater.
We observed that using BC-RNN as the inner loop policy yields high-quality active generation re-
sults, even when the final downstream policy is a Diffusion Policy. We found that using Diffusion
Policy directly within the meta-learning inner loop resulted in a “flat” rater score distribution, pre-
venting the network from effectively distinguishing aligned parameters. We hypothesize that this is
due to the stochastic noise inherent in the diffusion training, though further investigation is left for
future work. Consequently, we use BC-RNN as the inner policy for all experiments.
Importance of Co-Training the Rater. Empirically, we found it essential to input both simulation
and real-world data into the rater network during the inner loop updates (as shown in Algorithm 1).
If the rater is trained solely on the simulated probing dataset, it fails to correctly identify aligned
parameters, likely due to the lack of grounding in the real-world data distribution.

Policy Training and Evaluation
Simulation Policy Training. We employ BC-RNN [60] and Diffusion Policy [55] as our imitation
learning algorithms. Both architectures utilize a ResNet18 visual backbone. We also condition the
policies on language instructions using FiLM layers in the visual backbone. All policies are trained
for 500 epochs, with 500 gradient steps per epoch. Optimization is performed using the Adam opti-
mizer [58] with a learning rate of 10−4. The policy inputs consist of low-dimensional observations
(end-effector position, quaternion, and gripper position), RGB images (captured by agentview and
wrist-mounted cameras), and language embeddings. The low-dimensional observations are pro-
cessed by a MLP and concatenated with image features. Original input images of resolution 84×84
are randomly cropped to 76 × 76 during training. The policy output consists of the delta position
and rotation for the end-effector, as well as the gripper position. Hyperparameters used for policy
training are reported in Table 3.
Simulation Policy Evaluation. We evaluate policy checkpoints every 50 epochs, performing 50
rollouts per evaluation. For reporting, we select the peak success rate observed during training. All
experiments are repeated across three random seeds, with peak success rates reported independently
for each seed. In Figures 3 and 12, the bar heights represent the mean success rate across the three
seeds, while the error bars indicate the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM).

Real Policy Training. The training procedure for real-world policies mirrors the simulation setup,
with a few key distinctions. We exclusively utilize Diffusion Policy for all real-world experiments.
Additionally, we apply a Spatial Softmax layer [61] to the output of the visual encoders. The policy
input observations remain consistent with the simulation settings, with the exception of the wrist
camera image, which is omitted as it is not available on our real-world hardware. The policy output
consists of the absolute position and rotation of the end-effector, along with the gripper position.
