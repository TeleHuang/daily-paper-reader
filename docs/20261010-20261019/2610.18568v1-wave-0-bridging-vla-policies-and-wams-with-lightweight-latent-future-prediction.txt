Abstract
Vision–language–action (VLA) policies excel at generalist robotic manipulation but generate actions without
explicitly modeling their environmental consequences. World action models (WAMs) incorporate future
prediction, yet most rely on costly video generation, while latent alternatives either use predicted futures only
as auxiliary targets or infer latent actions from suboptimal visual transitions through costly large-scale video
pretraining. We introduce WAVE-0 (World-model-Augmented VLA Execution), a framework that bridges
VLAs and WAMs through a lightweight latent world model. By connecting the semantic understanding of
pretrained VLAs with explicit future prediction, WAVE-0 allows anticipated action consequences to directly
guide action generation. Built on a pretrained VLA, its 257M world model translates VLM-generated
latent action plans into predicted future representations that condition the action expert, unifying latent
planning, future prediction, and action generation. The world model is trained directly on ground-truth
robot actions with a counterfactual ranking objective, requiring neither pixel reconstruction nor large-scale
video pretraining. WAVE-0 achieves success rates of 91.47% on RoboTwin 2.0 and 98.7% on LIBERO,
along with 88.0%, 61.4%, and 75.0% on LIBERO-Plus, LIBERO-Pro, and LIBERO-Para, respectively. On
real-world tasks, it improves the success rate of 𝜋0.5 from 38.3% to 73.3%. These results demonstrate strong
manipulation performance and generalization across diverse distribution shifts.

Introduction
Vision–language–action (VLA) policies have made substantial progress in general robotic manipulation by combining
the semantic knowledge of pretrained vision–language models with action supervision from robot demonstrations [5–
8, 15, 17, 26]. While effective, this direct observation-to-action mapping does not explicitly capture how actions
transform the environment and thereby shape subsequent decisions. World action models (WAMs) address this limitation
by incorporating environmental dynamics into action generation, enabling policies to anticipate the future consequences
of their actions [4, 21, 38]. Most existing WAMs build on large-scale video generation models, whose pixel-level
prediction spends substantial computation on visual details irrelevant to action selection and limits inference efficiency.
Recent efforts have tried to reduce this overhead [10, 39], but they still rely on video generation backbones that devote
considerable capacity to visual reconstruction.

A promising alternative is to model environmental dynamics in a compact latent space, allowing future prediction to
focus on information relevant to action selection rather than visual reconstruction. VLA-JEPA [31] learns latent actions
through future-state prediction, while JEPA-WAM [24] uses a shared predictor to model state transitions and generate
actions. However, neither approach explicitly conditions action generation on predicted future states, leaving future
prediction and action selection only loosely connected. LaWAM [11] guides action generation with predicted latent
visual subgoals, but learns its latent actions indirectly from visual transitions through inverse dynamics. While this
formulation enables world model pretraining on large-scale videos without action annotations, the inferred latent actions
may summarize realized transitions rather than the executable actions that caused them. Consequently, a representation
that explains an observed transition may be misaligned with the action a policy must generate before the transition occurs.
Moreover, LaWAM pretrains its world model on approximately 4,500 hours of videos, followed by policy-integration
pretraining and additional benchmark-specific training, resulting in substantial training costs. This raises a natural
question: when domain-specific demonstrations already provide ground-truth executable actions, is such large-scale,
indirect pretraining truly more effective than directly training the world model with those actions?
We answer this question by grounding future prediction directly in the ground-truth actions of robot trajectories. Our
key insight is that demonstrations already pair each action sequence with its resulting observations, providing direct
supervision on how actions change the environment. A world model trained with this supervision predicts futures tied to
the executed actions rather than to latent actions inferred after the transition occurs, and requires no large-scale pretraining.
Moreover, since a pretrained VLA already provides rich semantic understanding and a visual representation space, the
world model only needs to predict how this representation evolves under a given action plan, which keeps it lightweight
and avoids reconstructing future images. We therefore view semantic understanding in VLAs and future prediction
in WAMs as complementary, where the former determines action intent and the latter anticipates its consequences.
Building on this view, we introduce WAVE-0 (World-model-Augmented VLA Execution), a framework that bridges VLAs
and WAMs through action-grounded latent future prediction. As shown in Figure 1, our latent world model transforms
VLM-generated latent action plans into predicted future visual representations that explicitly condition the action expert,
yielding a unified pipeline of latent planning, future prediction, and action generation.
WAVE-0 follows a two-stage training strategy. In the first stage, we train the latent world model on action-labeled
trajectories to predict future visual representations from current observations and ground-truth actions. Besides future
and latent delta objectives, a counterfactual ranking objective encourages the model to distinguish the consequences
of different action plans. In the second stage, we integrate the frozen world model into pretrained 𝜋0.5 [15] and align
VLM-generated latent plans with the learned action representation space. The world model predicts future representations
from these plans, and a lightweight resampler compresses them into future-conditioning tokens for the action expert. The
planning branch guides action generation only through these predictions, while the expert retains access to the original
VLM context. This design allows anticipated action consequences to explicitly guide action generation during both
training and inference.
On RoboTwin 2.0 [12] and LIBERO [25], WAVE-0 achieves average success rates of 91.47% and 98.7%, improving over
𝜋0.5 by 11.09 and 1.8 percentage points, respectively. On the more challenging LIBERO-Plus [13] and LIBERO-Pro [45],
which introduce variations in objects, layouts, camera viewpoints, and instructions, WAVE-0 achieves 88.0% and 61.4%,
outperforming all compared methods. On LIBERO-Para [16], it exceeds 𝜋0.5 by 3.6 percentage points under instruction
paraphrasing. These gains extend to three real-world tasks, where WAVE-0 improves the average success rate of 𝜋0.5
from 38.3% to 73.3%. Compared with LaWAM, WAVE-0 is also more training-efficient, processing far fewer training
examples across RoboTwin 2.0 and real-world tasks. Together, these results demonstrate that action-grounded latent
future prediction not only improves a strong VLA with modest cost, but also substantially enhances its robustness to
visual, spatial, and language distribution shifts.
Our contributions are threefold:
(1) We introduce WAVE-0, a framework that connects VLA semantic understanding with WAM future prediction through
a 257M lightweight latent world model, enabling anticipated action consequences to guide action generation during
training and inference.
(2) We develop a two-stage training strategy that learns action-dependent latent dynamics from ground-truth robot
actions using counterfactual ranking, then integrates the frozen world model into a pretrained VLA through an explicit
prediction bottleneck.

Related Works
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Policies
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies have advanced generalist robotic manipulation by combining pretrained vision-
language knowledge with robot action supervision [7, 8, 17, 18, 32, 35]. Diffusion-based and flow-based policies further
support expressive continuous action generation across diverse tasks and embodiments [5, 6, 15, 26, 33]. Beyond
action generation, embodied reasoning and explicit spatial representations have also been explored to enhance VLA
policies [30, 40]. Another line of work learns task-centric latent action representations from videos, enabling VLA
pretraining across embodiments with reduced reliance on action annotations [9, 34, 37]. Despite these advances, VLAs
primarily map observations and instructions to actions, leaving action-dependent environmental dynamics implicit. This
motivates integrating explicit future prediction into VLAs, allowing anticipated action consequences to directly guide
action generation.
2.2
Visual Generation-Based World Action Models
World action models (WAMs) incorporate future-state prediction into action generation to explicitly model how robot
actions affect the environment. Most existing WAMs build on pretrained video models that jointly predict future
observations and actions [4, 19, 21–23, 28, 38]. However, large backbones, modeling of action-irrelevant visual details,
and iterative denoising incur substantial computational cost and latency. To reduce this cost, Fast-WAM [39] and
GigaWorld-Policy [36] retain visual prediction only as training supervision and skip future generation at inference,
while other methods reuse future information through asynchronous execution, sparse interactions, or single-pass
3


caching [10, 42, 44]. Beyond video generation, BagelVLA [14] builds on a unified model for understanding and
generation to extract predictive visual features through single-step denoising, while ImageWAM [43] guides action
generation using denoising features from an image-editing model without decoding future images at inference. However,
visual generation backbones remain computationally demanding, motivating lightweight dynamics models that directly
predict future visual representations without pixel-level reconstruction.
2.3
Latent World Action Models
Joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) offers a framework for latent world action modeling by predicting
representations without pixel reconstruction [1, 2, 20, 27]. VLA-JEPA [31] learns action-relevant latent representations
from action-free human videos through world prediction, and then adapts them to robot control. JEPA-WAM [24] uses
spatially structured targets that jointly encode current and future observations, providing dense transition supervision for
policy learning. However, both methods use predictive supervision to shape policy representations without directly
conditioning action generation on predicted visual representations. LaWAM [11] learns latent actions through visual
inverse dynamics and uses them to predict visual subgoals that guide action generation. We posit that semantic
understanding in pretrained VLAs and future prediction in WAMs are complementary capabilities that should jointly
guide action generation. WAVE-0 bridges these paradigms through a lightweight latent world model that learns to predict
world state transitions from labeled robot action sequences. It translates VLM-generated latent action plans into future
representations that explicitly guide the action expert during both training and inference.

3.2
Model Architecture
Built on pretrained 𝜋0.5 [15], WAVE-0 combines vision-language understanding with explicit dynamics prediction through
latent action planning, future world state prediction, and future-conditioned action generation. The training procedure is
conducted in two sequential stages. In the first stage, the latent world model jointly learns action representations and
latent dynamics from visual observations, proprioceptive states, and ground-truth action sequences to predict future
world states in a fixed visual embedding space. In the second stage, we integrate the frozen world model into 𝜋0.5 and
train the VLM to produce latent action plans aligned with the learned action representation space. The world model
converts these plans into predicted future visual representations, which condition the action expert alongside the original
vision-language context.

Experiments
4.1
Implementation Details
We initialize the VLM and action expert from pretrained 𝜋0.5. For world modeling, we use a frozen copy of its SigLIP
encoder and visual projector to extract 2048-dimensional visual features, which are spatially pooled into a 4 × 4 grid,
yielding 16 tokens per camera. The latent world model processes these representations using a 12-layer Transformer
with a hidden dimension of 1024, 16 attention heads, and an MLP dimension of 4096. The resampler then pools the
predicted future representations to a 2 × 2 grid per camera and projects them into 12 conditioning tokens for the action
expert, which retains the original flow-matching formulation of 𝜋0.5.
The world model, action encoder, and state encoder together comprise approximately 257M trainable parameters in
Stage 1, while the full WAVE-0 contains approximately 4.3B parameters. We train with AdamW using a default peak
learning rate of 10−4, weight decay of 0.01, and a cosine learning rate schedule. Benchmark-specific training and
inference configurations are provided in Appendix A.1.

4.2
Evaluation on LIBERO
We evaluate on the four LIBERO suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, covering 40 tasks. Our training data comprise
1,693 trajectories and 273,465 frames across these suites. As shown in Table 1a, WAVE-0 achieves an average success
rate of 98.7%, the highest among the compared methods, improving over 𝜋0.5 by 1.8 percentage points. The largest
gain occurs on the Long suite, where success increases from 92.4% to 97.6%. This suggests that anticipating action
consequences is particularly beneficial for sequential manipulation, where the outcome of each action affects subsequent
steps. WAVE-0 also achieves 100.0% on Object and 98.6% on Goal, supporting the effectiveness of latent future
conditioning across diverse manipulation tasks.
4.3
Evaluation on RoboTwin 2.0
RoboTwin 2.0 is a bimanual manipulation benchmark with 50 tasks. Following prior work [4, 21, 39], we train a single
policy on all tasks using 2,500 demonstrations collected in clean scenes and 25,000 demonstrations collected under
heavy domain randomization.
As shown in Table 1b, WAVE-0 achieves an average success rate of 91.47%, improving over 𝜋0.5 by 11.09 percentage
points, with consistent gains in both clean (81.12% to 91.46%) and randomized (79.64% to 91.48%) scenes. Compared
with LaWAM, which also models dynamics in latent space, WAVE-0 achieves a higher average success rate (91.47% vs.
91.22%) and remains more robust under randomization (91.48% vs. 89.80%). Per-task results in Appendix Table 13
further show large gains over 𝜋0.5 on long-horizon tasks with multiple dependent subgoals, such as Stack Blocks Three
(+23.0) and Stack Bowls Three (+33.0), suggesting that anticipating the consequences of latent action plans benefits
sequential manipulation.

4.4
Out-of-Distribution Generalization
We evaluate generalization on LIBERO-Plus, LIBERO-Pro, and LIBERO-Para, directly using the model trained on
LIBERO without additional fine-tuning. LIBERO-Plus evaluates robustness across seven perturbation categories,
including camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting, backgrounds, sensor noise, and object
layouts. As shown in Table 2, WAVE-0 achieves an overall success rate of 88.0%, outperforming all compared methods
and exceeding 𝜋0.5 by 3.5 percentage points. It improves performance in six of the seven categories, with the largest
gains under robot initial-state perturbations (+14.0) and camera viewpoint changes (+6.5).
On LIBERO-Pro, which measures generalization under changes to objects, spatial configurations, instructions, and
environments, WAVE-0 achieves 61.4% success, the highest among the compared methods and an 8.4-point improvement
over 𝜋0.5. We further assess robustness to instruction paraphrasing on LIBERO-Para, where WAVE-0 improves success
from 71.4% to 75.0%. Together, these results indicate that latent future conditioning improves generalization under
visual, spatial, and linguistic distribution shifts.

4.6
Analysis
Training Efficiency. WAVE-0 learns latent dynamics from action-labeled target demonstrations without large-scale video
pretraining. In contrast, LaWAM performs 100k steps of world model pretraining and 200k steps of policy-integration
pretraining with a global batch size of 1,024, processing 307.2M examples before benchmark-specific training. On
RoboTwin 2.0, the two-stage training of WAVE-0 requires approximately 670 H100 GPU hours and processes 76.8M
examples, compared with 1,280 H100 GPU hours and 102.4M examples for LaWAM’s benchmark-specific training
alone. Across three real-world tasks, WAVE-0 processes 33.28M examples, compared with 345.60M for LaWAM,
including shared pretraining and task-specific policy training. Amortized per task, these budgets are 11.09M and
115.20M, respectively, with WAVE-0 achieving higher success rates, as shown in Tables 4 and 3. These results support
the efficiency of action-grounded latent world modeling without additional large-scale video pretraining.
Attention Visualization. Figure 3(b) compares attention from action queries to current main-view image patches at the
final denoising step. In the illustrated examples, WAVE-0 exhibits more localized attention around the manipulated
object and target bottle, whereas LaWAM shows more dispersed responses over the robot arm, image boundaries, and
background regions.

4.7
Ablation Studies
4.7.1
Effects of World Model Integration Strategy
We investigate how the learned world model should be integrated into the policy by comparing 𝜋0.5, 𝜋0.5 + WM, and
WAVE-0 on RoboTwin 2.0. As illustrated in Figure 1, 𝜋0.5 + WM uses the world model to predict future representations
from generated actions and provides an auxiliary training loss against the observed future. In contrast, WAVE-0 explicitly
conditions action generation on predicted futures during both training and inference. Both variants use the same frozen
world model trained in Stage 1.
As shown in Table 1b, auxiliary supervision improves average success from 80.38% to 89.51%, supporting the value of
the learned latent dynamics for policy learning. WAVE-0 further increases success to 91.47%, outperforming 𝜋0.5 +
WM by 1.98 and 1.94 percentage points in clean and randomized settings, respectively. These results indicate that while
world model supervision already yields substantial gains, explicitly conditioning action generation on predicted futures
provides consistent additional benefits. More broadly, they support the central premise of WAM that anticipating the
consequences of actions improves action generation, and show that this benefit can be realized in a compact latent space
without video generation.

5
Conclusion
We presented WAVE-0, a framework that bridges VLA policies and world action models through a lightweight latent
world model. Instead of generating future frames or using predicted futures only as auxiliary training targets, WAVE-0
translates VLM-generated latent action plans into predicted future visual representations that explicitly condition the
action expert during both training and inference. The world model is grounded in ground-truth robot actions and
trained with counterfactual ranking supervision, which encourages action-dependent dynamics and improves policy
generalization. Built on 𝜋0.5, WAVE-0 substantially improves performance on simulation and real-world benchmarks
without large-scale world model pretraining, suggesting that grounding latent dynamics in executable actions offers an
efficient path toward world-model-augmented robot policies.

ImageWAM.
For each real-robot task, ImageWAM is independently trained on the corresponding 200 demonstrations
for 60k optimization steps. Training is performed on eight GPUs with a per-GPU batch size of 16, resulting in a global
batch size of 128. We train the action prediction module using BF16 mixed precision and the AdamW optimizer. The
learning rate is linearly warmed up for 3,000 steps, corresponding to 5% of the total training budget, and then decayed
using a cosine schedule. ImageWAM jointly learns visual dynamics and action prediction from the three camera views
and produces a 16-step absolute joint-action chunk during inference. The main training parameters are summarized in
Table 10.
