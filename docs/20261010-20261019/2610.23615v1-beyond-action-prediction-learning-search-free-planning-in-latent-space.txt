## ABSTRACT 

Latent world models learn action-conditioned state transitions in a compact rep-resentation space, providing a predictive model for visual planning and control. However, planning with these models typically requires costly test-time optimiza-tion over action sequences. We propose to replace this online search with a goal-conditioned inverse model that directly predicts actions from current and goal observations. A key challenge is that action prediction alone neither ensures that goal representations encode action-relevant information nor that predicted actions are consistent with the learned latent prediction model. We introduce Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), a search-free framework that addresses both as-pects during training. HIJP relabels future observations along offline trajectories as goals, providing action supervision across multiple temporal horizons. By re-taining gradients through the goal representations, this supervision directly shapes the visual representation. HIJP further feeds predicted actions into the latent pre-dictor model and aligns the resulting next-state representations with encoded suc-cessor observations, enforcing action-transition consistency. At inference time, the inverse dynamics model and latent predictor models are applied alternately to generate multi-step action sequences without online search. Across four vi-sual control tasks, HIJP achieves 86.8% overall success and a 1.9–16.5× planning speedup over search-based planning. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Latent world models encode high-dimensional visual observations into compact latent representa-tions and learn an action-conditioned latent predictor from offline trajectories, providing a founda-tion for visual planning and control (Zhou et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). How-ever, planning with these models typically requires optimizers such as CEM (Rubinstein, 1999) or MPPI (Williams et al., 2017) to repeatedly generate and evaluate candidate action sequences at each replanning step, which requires substantial test-time computation (Pinneri et al., 2021). Learning a direct mapping from the current state and goal to actions therefore provides a promising alternative for improving the planning efficiency of latent world models (Sun et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2026). Recent work has introduced action supervision to improve latent world models’ control capabili-ties. SMWM learns action-relevant representations through inverse dynamics supervision (Ivashkov et al., 2026), while Delta-JEPA (Zhang et al., 2026) recovers actions from latent state differences, encouraging representations to retain action-related state changes. GC-IDM (Nguyen et al., 2026) further learns a goal-conditioned action mapping on frozen representations, while INTACT (Sun et al., 2026)incorporates local and future-goal action supervision into end-to-end training, support-ing search-free recursive control. These approaches demonstrate the value of action supervision for goal-conditioned control. However, accurate action prediction alone does not necessarily provide all the supervision needed for search-free planning. We identify two aspects that remain underconstrained. First, goal-conditioned action supervision should not only train an inverse model to predict actions, but also help shape visual representations 1arXiv:2610.23615v1 [cs.RO] 16 Oct 2026 to capture how actions relate current states to future goals. Offline trajectories naturally contain such relations at multiple temporal scales: for the same current state and demonstrated action, dif-ferent future observations define goals at different temporal horizons. However, existing training schemes (Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a) exploit only a subset of these relations, for exam-ple by conditioning action prediction on a fixed future endpoint. More generally, treating the goal representation only as a conditioning target limits the extent to which action supervision can shape goal-side visual features. Second, accurate action prediction alone does not ensure that the predicted action induces the correct transition under the learned latent predictor. The inverse dynamics model and the latent predictor model are typically supervised separately, with the latter conditioned on demonstrated actions (Ivashkov et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). During search-free rollout planning, however, these two mappings are applied sequentially: the inverse dynamics model predicts an ac-tion toward the goal, and the latent predictor model uses this predicted action to advance the latent state for subsequent action generation. The resulting inverse-to-predictor composition is therefore directly involved in planning, yet is not explicitly constrained by separate action and latent predic-tion objectives. This motivates extending action supervision beyond action prediction itself, using it both to shape goal-conditioned representations and to couple predicted actions with their induced latent transitions. Based on this principle, we propose Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), which jointly trains a visual encoder, a goal-conditioned inverse dynamics model, and a latent predictor model end-to-end. HIJP relabels future observations within each trajectory window as goals, exposing the inverse ob-jective to diverse state–goal relations across temporal horizons. Gradients are retained through the goal representations, allowing inverse-action supervision to directly shape the visual features used for goal-conditioned action prediction. We further feed the predicted actions into the latent predictor and align the resulting successor latents with the encodings of the next observations. This action-transition consistency objective jointly updates the inverse and predictor models, directly coupling action generation with its induced latent transition. In this way, HIJP trains the same inverse-to-predictor mapping that is repeatedly applied during planning. At test time, the inverse dynamics module and latent predictor model alternate to generate multi-step action sequences, which are exe-cuted through closed-loop replanning without online action search. We evaluate HIJP on four visual control tasks under four configurations of goal temporal offsets and execution budgets. HIJP achieves an overall success rate of 86 .8% , outperforming the strongest baseline by 4.9 percentage points. Ablations on hindsight goal sampling, training objectives, and gradient routing examine how goal-side representation learning and inverse - predictor coupling contribute to control performance. Beyond success rates, we also evaluate efficiency and the tem-poral smoothness of executed actions. With matched numbers of replanning calls, HIJP achieves a 

1.9–16 .5× planning speedup over LeWM. Under the most challenging setting, its executed actions exhibit lower jerk than those produced by CEM using the same representations, indicating smoother executed controls. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Latent World Models Latent world models learn action-conditioned changes in representation space to support goal-directed planning from offline visual trajectories. Existing approaches span pretrained visual features, end-to-end representation learning, and large-scale video pretraining (Zhou et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026; Assran et al., 2025; Bo et al., 2026). Systematic studies further investigate how architectures, training objectives, and planning algorithms affect control perfor-mance (Terver et al., 2025). Gao & Xu (2026) reduce forward-rollout costs through parallel predic-tion conditioned on action prefixes. Wang et al. (2026a) use inverse dynamics and goal-conditioned action supervision to improve planning representations, while retaining CEM for control. HIJP builds on latent forward dynamics and focuses on learning a goal-conditioned action mapping that can be directly deployed for control. 

Inverse Dynamics and Goal-Conditioned Action Learning Inverse dynamics and action-supervised objectives encourage representations to retain action-relevant state changes. These ob-jectives can also be combined with physical-state alignment to improve representations for control (Ivashkov et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Hindsight learning relabels achieved states along trajectories as goals, providing supervision for goal-conditioned policies and inverse dy-2namics learning (Andrychowicz et al., 2018; Ghosh et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2019). Goal-conditioned action-sequence prediction has also been used for visual planning (Paster et al., 2020). Related theoretical studies analyze the benefits of inverse dynamics through bias–variance trade-offs and learning-problem complexity (Sch¨ afer et al., 2026; Morin et al., 2026). Within latent world models, GC-IDM(Nguyen et al., 2026) learns a goal- and horizon-conditioned action mapping on frozen representations, while INTACT (Sun et al., 2026) jointly learns local and goal-conditioned action prediction to support search-free recursive control. HIJP follows this direction by enriching super-vision through future-goal relabeling within trajectory windows. 

Learned and Amortized Planning Planning computation can be shifted to training by learning action priors, latent subgoals, or plan-update rules (Wang et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2026; Masip et al., 2026; Sommer & Schilling, 2026; Tang et al., 2025). Another line of work first predicts future states or generates state trajectories and then recovers actions through inverse dynamics (Tian et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026; Ajay et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2026). Rakhimov et al. (2026) jointly train state and action flows, allowing a single model to support multiple control infer-ence modes. HIJP alternates goal-conditioned action prediction with forward latent-state prediction to construct multi-step plans without online action search. 

Consistency and Goal-Aware Planning Objectives Consistency objectives also connect model prediction with planning. Seo et al. (2026) incorporate action-recovery errors from predicted transi-tions into the online planning cost, while Bai & Xiong (2026) combine temporal-progress learning with multi-step rollout consistency supervision. HIJP focuses on the composed output of the goal-conditioned action module and the forward model. During training, it aligns the successor latent state predicted under the generated action with the encoding of the next observation in the recorded trajectory, allowing state-prediction errors to supervise action learning directly. 

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

Our goal is to extend action supervision beyond action prediction itself, such that it also shapes goal-conditioned visual representations and constrains the latent transitions induced by predicted actions. HIJP jointly learns a visual encoder fθ , a latent predictor model gϕ, and a goal-conditioned inverse dynamics model hξ . Given offline visual trajectories, the inverse dynamics model learns to predict actions toward future goals, while the predictor predicts action-conditioned latent transitions. We couple these components during training to supervise the local inverse-to-predictor composition repeatedly used in search-free planning. Figure 1 provides an overview. 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION

and a goal observation og , the task is to produce actions that reach the goal within a fixed interaction budget. Training uses recorded observations and actions, with future observations in each trajectory serving as hindsight goals, where reward annotations are not required. Our planning procedure repeatedly composes two mappings. An inverse model predicts an action from the current latent state and goal latent state, and a latent predictor model predicts the latent state induced by that action. This composition motivates training the models together: action prediction must identify a useful action, while the resulting transition must agree with the observed dynamics. 3.2 LATENT WORLD MODEL

A trajectory contains multiple valid future goals for a given observation. To expose these relations during training, we sample a window of W observations, an anchor i and a future offset κ:

i ∼ Unif {0, . . . , W − 2}, κ | i ∼ Unif {1, . . . , W − 1 − i}. (4) Each sampled pair therefore defines a goal-conditioned action-learning problem at a potentially different temporal horizon. Unlike using only the endpoint of a trajectory window, this construction exposes the inverse objective to diverse relations between current observations and future goals using the same offline data. The observation at i + κ becomes the hindsight goal. The goal-conditioned inverse model predicts the demonstrated action at the anchor: 

ˆai = hξ (zi, z i+κ), Linv = MAE(ˆ ai, a i). (5) Although the sampled goal may be several steps ahead, the target is the immediate action ai, not the intervening action sequence. Each training window therefore supplies action supervision under goals at different temporal offsets. Gradients from Linv flow through both zi and zi+κ. Consequently, the inverse objective trains not only hξ , but also the visual features used to represent the current observation and the future goal. We implement hξ as an MLP operating on the concatenated latent pair. 43.4 ACTION TRANSITION CONSISTENCY 

The inverse model can predict an action close to the demonstrated action without ensuring that this prediction produces the observed transition under the learned predictor model. This distinction matters at planning time, where the model receives predicted rather than demonstrated actions. We therefore pass the predicted action through gϕ:

˜zi+1 = gϕ(zi, ˆai) , ˆai = hξ (zi, z i+κ), (6) and align the result with the encoded one-step successor: 

Lcyc = MSE(˜ zi+1 , sg[ zi+1 ]) , (7) where sg denotes stop-gradient. This objective uses the one-step successor zi+1 even when the hindsight goal zi+κ is farther away. It allows Lcyc to update the inverse head, action encoder, latent prediction model, and the anchor-side visual representation. Thus, the consistency objective does not merely use the prediction model to provide an error signal to the inverse dynamics model; it jointly adapts action generation and the latent predictor around their composed prediction. This directly supervises the inverse-to-latent predictor transition that is repeatedly composed during search-free planning. 3.5 JOINT OBJECTIVE AND SEARCH -FREE PLANNING 

Joint training. The full training objective combines latent prediction, representation regularization, hindsight-conditioned action prediction, and action-transition consistency: 

L = Lpred + λreg Lsigreg + λinv Linv + λcyc Lcyc . (8) All components are optimized jointly, and all terms are averaged over their corresponding batch, temporal, sampled-pair, and feature dimensions before weighting. For all tasks, the loss weights are set to λreg = 0 .09 , λinv = 1 .0, and λcyc = 0 .05 for TwoRoom and 0.10 for the remaining tasks. 

Search-free planning. At planning time, we use the inverse dynamics model and latent prediction models in the same order in which they are coupled during training. At each replanning point, the latest observation and fixed goal observation are encoded as zt = fθ (ot) and zg = fθ (og ). Starting from ˆz0 = zt, we construct an H-step plan by alternating goal-conditioned action prediction and latent prediction:

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), a search-free framework for goal-conditioned visual control. HIJP samples future observations as hindsight goals to enrich inverse-dynamics supervision and allows training gradients to update goal representations. It further feeds predicted actions into a latent predictor model and constrains the resulting latent transitions through a consistency objective. At planning time, the inverse and latent predictor models alternate to construct multi-step action plans without online search. Across four visual control tasks and multiple eval-uation configurations, HIJP achieves the highest aggregate goal-reaching performance among the evaluated methods while reducing planning time compared to a search-based world-model planner. Under the most challenging setting, its executed actions are also smoother than those produced by CEM using the same representations. Together, these results suggest that aligning goal-conditioned action learning with the latent transitions used during planning provides an effective route toward efficient, search-free visual control. 96 LIMITATIONS 

Our evaluation focuses on tasks with a limited temporal span, using fixed planning horizons and replanning schedules. Extending HIJP to substantially longer-horizon tasks remains an important direction. Future work will explore hierarchical subgoal generation, adaptive planning horizons, and dynamic replanning schedules. These extensions aim to decompose longer tasks into shorter goal-conditioned segments and adapt planning and execution intervals to the requirements of each segment.
