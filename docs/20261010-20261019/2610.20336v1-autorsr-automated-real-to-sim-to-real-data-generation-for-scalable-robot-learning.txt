# AutoRSR: Automated Real-to-Sim-to-Real Data Generation for Scalable Robot Learning 

Jiawei Zhang, Chang Liu, Litao Deng, Tao Zhang, Qin Jin 

Renmin University of China 

Abstract — Scaling embodied robot learning requires large and diverse manipulation datasets, but collecting real-world demonstrations remains expensive in human effort and robot time. Simulation provides a scalable alternative, yet converting a real workspace into a robot-learning-ready environment still requires substantial manual effort for object extraction, asset processing, coordinate calibration, and trajectory specification. We present AutoRSR, a highly automated real-to-sim-to-real framework that converts scanned real-world workspaces into reusable embodied simulation assets and scalable manipula-tion data. Given multi-view observations, robot states, and open-vocabulary semantic queries, AutoRSR reconstructs the workspace, extracts individual object assets, and automatically registers the reconstructed scene to the robot base frame at met-ric scale. Starting from a small number of reference motions, AutoRSR then generates executable manipulation trajectories and scales them through trajectory interpolation and domain randomization.After workspace scanning and semantic queries, the remaining pipeline requires no manual object annotation, scene-to-robot calibration, or robot teleoperation. Across three real-world manipulation tasks, policies trained on 1K automat-ically generated episodes per task achieve 73.3–86.7% real-world success rates, comparable to π0.5 trained from 150 human teleoperated demonstrations, while reducing the human time required for data generation by an order of magnitude. These results demonstrate that real-world observations can be converted into metrically aligned, executable, and scalable simulation data with substantially reduced human effort. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A major bottleneck in embodied robot learning is the lack of scalable and diverse manipulation data. Real-world teleoperation provides high-fidelity demonstrations but re-quires substantial human effort and robot time, whereas simulation can generate large-scale data at much lower cost. The challenge, however, is not simply to create a visually realistic simulation. For simulation to serve as a practical data engine, a real workspace must be converted into a robot-learning-ready embodied environment : its objects should be individually reusable, its geometry should remain metrically consistent with the physical workspace, and its manipula-tion trajectories should be executable and scalable without repeatedly involving a human operator. Existing approaches for constructing robot-learning sim-ulations can be broadly categorized into generative and reconstruction-based methods. Generative approaches [1], [2] offer flexible scene and asset synthesis, but the generated environments may differ from the geometry and appearance of a target workspace. Reconstruction-based approaches [3], [4], [5], [6], [7] , in contrast, preserve the visual and spatial characteristics of real environments and are therefore attrac-tive for sim-to-real robot learning. However, when recon-struction is used as a data generation engine, substantial hu-man intervention remains between scene reconstruction and executable robot learning data. In particular, the remaining effort arises from two coupled challenges: preparing reusable simulation assets and making the reconstructed environment executable by a robot. On reusable asset construction : Obtaining simulation-ready objects from a real workspace typically involves sep-arate object and scene scanning [3], manual object segmen-tation [4], or additional per-object processing for extraction and completion [7]. Such operations introduce object-level human effort that grows with scene complexity and must be repeated when the workspace changes. More importantly, these pipelines generally treat scene reconstruction and ob-ject preparation as separate stages rather than providing a unified mechanism for turning a scanned workspace into reusable manipulation assets. On executable trajectory generation : A reconstructed scene alone is also insufficient for robot policy learning. The reconstruction is typically represented in its own coordinate system and must be registered to the robot before trajectories can be executed. This registration often requires explicit scene-to-robot calibration or manual alignment. Moreover, demonstrations used for learning are commonly collected through real-robot teleoperation, or trajectories must be manually specified and adapted to the reconstructed scene. Consequently, even when simulation removes the cost of physically executing thousands of demonstrations, human effort remains embedded in the process of producing the initial executable data. This limits the extent to which reconstruction-based simulation can serve as an automated and reusable data engine. We address these limitations with AutoRSR , a highly automated real-to-sim-to-real framework that converts ascanned real workspace into scalable manipulation data. The key idea is to exploit information already available during workspace scanning to eliminate subsequent manual alignment and annotation. We perform a hybrid scan of the workspace while recording wrist-camera poses and robot joint states. Given open-vocabulary semantic queries, these observations are used to reconstruct the workspace and extract individual object assets without manually annotated masks. In summary, our main contributions are: (i) We present 

AutoRSR , an automated real-to-sim-to-real pipeline that converts scanned real workspaces into reusable, metrically aligned, and robot-executable simulation environments for manipulation data generation. (ii) We introduce an au-tomated processing for open-vocabulary object extraction, scene-to-robot registration, and executable trajectory genera-tion, enabling large-scale simulation data generation without manual object annotation, manual coordinate alignment, or robot teleoperation. (iii) We demonstrate the effectiveness of AutoRSR on three real-world manipulation tasks. Policies trained on 1K automatically generated episodes achieve real-robot performance comparable to policies trained on 150 human teleoperated demonstrations, while substantially reducing human data-collection effort. II. R ELATED WORKS 

A. Robot Data Collection Paradigms. 

The most prevalent source of robot manipulation data today is human teleoperation data[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. However, collecting such data requires extensive human effort and time, making large-scale data acquisition prohibitively expensive. To obtain large volumes of robot manipulation data at a lower cost, researchers have explored various data generation paradigms. Ego-centric[15], [16] and humanoid-based approaches[17], [18] consider human be-haviors as a rich source of robot data. They enable robots to learn object interactions or joint motions through first-person hand manipulation data or human motion capture. UMI-based approaches[19], [20] enable fast and convenient col-lection of in-the-wild manipulation demonstrations by using a handheld gripper for human demonstrations and recovering the corresponding trajectories. Nevertheless, the embodi-ment gap between humans and robots remains a significant challenge. In contrast, generating large-scale data through simulation augmentation offers the most cost-effective and convenient solution. MimicGen[21], [22] generates diverse trajectories by transferring, perturbing, replaying, and com-posing a small number of human demonstrations. Other ap-proaches, such as RoboGen[23] and HRIBench[24], leverage large language models to plan behaviors and automatically generate simulated data. However, the sim-to-real gap intro-duced by simulation still limits the effectiveness of such data for training real-world robots. 

B. Real2Sim2Real Data Generation. 

To reduce the sim-to-real gap of simulation data, many ap-proaches have begun to follow the Real2Sim2Real paradigm ,which reconstructs the real world from observations, imports the reconstructed environments into simulators, and then collects and augments large-scale data in simulation. After training, the learned policies are transferred back to the real world for inference[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. With the rapid development of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)[25] and the increasing support for neural Gaussian representa-tions in modern simulators, the visual sim-to-real gap has been significantly reduced. As a result, many approaches have started to build digital twins[26] by combining neural Gaussian representations of objects for high visual fidelity with collision mesh representations for physical interactions within simulation environments. III. M ETHOD 

We perform hybrid scene scanning using the robot-mounted wrist camera and third-party cameras while record-ing the robot states. We then use a head-mounted camera to capture a human manipulation demonstration video. Then we perform anchor-based semantic reconstruction. After separat-ing the objects and the scene according to the user’s open-vocabulary semantic queries, we import them separately into the simulator. We then align the robot coordinate system with the reconstruction coordinate system, retarget the robot manipulation trajectory based on the demonstration video, and augment the aligned trajectories to generate a large-scale dataset. Users only need to provide scene-scanning data, a demonstration video, and open-vocabulary semantic queries for the target objects; the entire remaining pipeline is then executed fully automatically.

We then established a mapping between image filenames and the corresponding robot poses to support subsequent alignment. 2) Object Extraction and Asset Generation: Given a user query, we use GroundingDINO[35] and SAM to obtain a target mask on a rendered view, then associate it with reconstructed instance IDs through overlap scoring against the rendered semantic-ID map. We extract the corresponding anchors according to the selected IDs. For object removal, we expand the removal region through spatial neighbor-hoods to suppress residual related Gaussians, yielding both 

object-only and scene-without-object representations. Since anchor-based Gaussian attributes are view-dependent, we evaluate their MLPs from multiple training cameras and aggregate the decoded attributes into standard explicit 3DGS assets. Object export uses opacity-based cross-view consen-sus and median aggregation, while scene export averages col-ors and opacities over accepted views to preserve coverage. When necessary, the explicit Gaussians are further distilled against multi-view renders from the original anchor model to reduce conversion artifacts. To obtain simulation geometry, we use SuGaR[36] to reconstruct meshes from the explicit Gaussians. For isolated objects, anisotropic Gaussian support removes unsupported mesh faces, followed by voxelization and surface reconstruc-tion to generate watertight collision meshes. The demonstration is interpreted using the calibrated head-camera pose, rather than assigning it to an arbitrary scanning image. 

2) Object Tracking and Grasp Retargeting: Using the open-vocabulary semantic queries already provided by the user, we employ GroundingDINO[35] and SAM2[31] to locate and segment the target object in the first frame of the human demonstration video, then propagate its mask throughout the video. Since all required inputs have already been prepared in the preceding stages, this process is fully automated. We then recover the object’s motion in 3D from the 2D demonstration video through differentiable Gaussian rendering. Gaussian appearance and local geometry remain fixed while the object pose is optimized using a fore-ground–background balanced silhouette loss, Dice loss, and robust foreground photometric error. Temporal regulariza-tion and a bounded image-centroid prior discourage abrupt changes in tracking. Initialization is constrained to a neigh-borhood of the reconstructed object pose, and subsequent frames use the preceding estimate as their initial guess. Next, we extract object geometry from the collision mesh and generate grasp candidates with different approach di-rections, gripper-closing directions, and positions. Candidate generation accounts for the gripper’s opening range and the object geometry. We use image-space hand cues pro-vided by HaMeR [38] to rank grasp candidates according to how closely their projected grasp locations match the thumb–index midpoint in the demonstration. These cues guide grasp selection without treating monocular hand-depth estimates as metric ground truth. We plan both the gripper opening at contact and its opening during approach. The desired end-effector motion is determined by the tracked object motion and the selected grasp pose in the object’s local frame. Inverse kinematics (IK) with joint-limit constraints is solved using sequential warm starts along the trajectory, and candidate acceptance checks both position and orientation residuals over the com-plete sequence. Robot forward kinematics, visual meshes, and collision geometry use the same URDF link transforms. During rigid-object transport with the gripper fully closed, the object pose is bound to the achieved end-effector pose while preserving the local grasp transform. Gaussian and collision geometry therefore move together despite finite IK residuals. At release, the achieved placement pose is retained, rather than snapping the object back to the nominal trajectory or allowing it to follow the retreating gripper. For constrained articulated or pushing motions, the prescribed object kine-matics are retained instead, and contact is checked against the achieved robot motion. 

D. Motion Trajectory and Visual Augmentation 1) Motion Trajectory Augmentation: We construct areusable bank of feasible motion templates before expanding appearance diversity. We independently randomize the table-top placements of all objects specified for segmentation by the open-vocabulary semantic queries. We also modify their geometric properties. Planar translations are sampled from a bounded distribution, while yaw rotations are applied about each object’s initial position rather than the world origin. Initial heights remain unchanged, and the system preserves the validated grasp relationships and object scales. Independent placement is coupled with task-relative trajec-tory adaptation. The manipulated object retains its sampled initial pose, while its terminal pose is adjusted to preserve the demonstrated relationship to the independently placed re-ceiver. A smooth time-dependent correction blends positions directly in world coordinates and interpolates the relative orientation. An explicitly specified interaction interval can enforce the relationship throughout that interval instead of only at the endpoint. This preserves the demonstrated motion structure while avoiding unintended orbital motion around the coordinate origin. Each candidate is expanded into a synchronized approach, gripper-closing, and task-motion sequence. For placement tasks, a geometric support test determines whether a short settling motion, gripper opening, and retreat can be ap-pended. Support is assessed using object-relative surface probes, so a common planar transformation does not change the verdict. An object without confirmed terminal support remains held and required-placement candidates are rejected if support cannot be established. Settling is a bounded contact adjustment, not randomized vertical placement. Motion candidates are solved in batches, with sequential warm starts within each trajectory. We screen grasp support, joint limits, and pose residuals, and recheck terminal support after binding rigid objects to achieved forward kinematics. Replay additionally checks finger contact during fully closed intervals and excessive robot–object mesh penetration. Col-lision meshes follow their corresponding Gaussian assets but contribute no object appearance. These checks provide geometric feasibility screening, rather than a guarantee of force closure or dynamic task success. 

2) Visual Augmentation: We combine the large number of augmented motion templates with multiple visual config-urations. While keeping the geometry, motion, actions, and timing unchanged, we randomize the textures of both objects and scenes, and apply coherent adjustments to the Gaussians’ exposure and white balance. Lighting variation is applied to the robot mesh renderer. During rendering, we continuously reuse Genesis[40], [41] runtime resources and cached Gaussian assets, and batch-process both Gaussian data and camera requests for compatible episodes. Robot meshes and Gaussian fore-ground/background layers are rendered from consistent cam-era poses and composited using depth. The head camera remains fixed in the rig frame, while wrist-camera poses are recomputed from the corresponding robot joint configura-tions and hand–eye transformations. All outputs share a single episode timeline: camera ob-servations, proprioceptive states, robot actions, and gripper openings all use aligned frame indices and timestamps. Each episode contains the observations and actions required for training. The observation views retain the input robot wrist-camera view and head-camera view. IV. E XPERIMENTS

We perform hybrid scene scanning using the built-in camera of a handheld smartphone and two wrist-mounted D405 cameras. In addition, a D435 camera is mounted at a fixed position in the scene to provide a top-down view and record human demonstration videos. In this paper, we present AutoRSR, a highly automated simulation data reconstruction and augmentation framework. The proposed system efficiently reconstructs real-world scenes, extracts object assets through semantic segmenta-tion, aligns manipulation trajectories, and generates large-scale augmented data, enabling cost-effective acquisition of high-quality simulation data for policy learning.
