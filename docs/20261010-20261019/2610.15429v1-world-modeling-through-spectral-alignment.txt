## ABSTRACT 

How do we tell a world model what information to preserve? We study this ques-tion through the lens of spectral representation learning. We introduce SpecWM, a JEPA framework that explicitly specifies desired relationships between observa-tions through a target similarity kernel and trains the encoder to reproduce those relationships in its embedding geometry. Different kernels provide different su-pervision, ranging from physical state information to a fully self-supervised tem-poral kernel requiring only the ordering of observations within trajectories. We characterize the global optima of the objective, establish conditions for linear re-coverability and invariance to nuisance information, and connect temporal-kernel similarity to the behavioral successor measure under an idealized setting. Em-pirically, the choice of kernel strongly controls which physical quantities are re-coverable from the representation. More importantly, temporal spectral alignment improves CEM planning success over LeWorldModel from 52% to 68% on OG-Bench Cube, 49% to 77% on OGBench Scene, and 29% to 51% on CALVIN. These results suggest that explicitly specifying representation geometry provides a simple way to steer what latent world models preserve for control. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The goal of a world model is to encode sufficient information to predict the consequences of an agent’s actions (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018). At the same time, complex real-world observations contain large amounts of task-irrelevant information (Zhang et al., 2021). Learning a compact rep-resentation therefore hinges on deciding which aspects of an observation to retain. Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs; LeCun, 2022) predict outcomes in a latent space, rather than recon-structing high-dimensional observations in pixel space, allowing learned representations to abstract away details of the input. Due to the lack of explicit supervision or grounding in raw observation space, training JEPAs is generally non-trivial. The training objective must implicitly guide which distinctions the model preserves without requiring it to reproduce every detail of an observation. Recent work has made substantial progress toward stable JEPA training through regularizing the embedding distribution (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025; Maes et al., 2026). However, avoiding collapse alone does not ensure that a representation preserves information needed for control, and the degree to which this happens is dictated implicitly by self-prediction (Tang et al., 2023). For instance, an encoder may distinguish robot configurations while ignoring the objects the robot must manipulate. We seek a direct method to express these relationships and understand their consequences. We approach this problem through the lens of spectral representation learning (Belkin & Niyogi, 2003; Bengio et al., 2004), which historically focuses on extracting representations from pairwise relationships between samples. Prior work has shown that several contrastive and non-contrastive objectives generally admit spectral interpretations (HaoChen et al., 2021; Balestriero & LeCun, 2022). Building on this perspective, we make the pairwise notion of similarity between two obser-vations the center of a new training objective for latent dynamics modeling. We introduce a spectral alignment framework for training world models to preserve a chosen sim-ilarity structure. Leveraging a notion of distances between observations (the teacher ), we define a target similarity kernel. We then train an encoder (the student ) to learn embeddings whose pairwise

We name the resulting world model SpecWM. Theoretically, we first study how the choice of teacher shapes the learned representation. This connection allows us to identify which quantities can be recovered from the rep-resentation via a linear probe. When teacher supervision is only available within a trajectory, we establish conditions under which matching the teacher removes observation noise from the embed-dings. Further, when leveraging a self-supervised temporal teacher, whose only source of supervision is the order of observations in a trajectory, the resulting world models outperform existing JEPA world models (Maes et al., 2026) in planning performance. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 1. We introduce a novel world modeling objective based on spectral alignment. 2. We formally study this framework through the lens of spectral representation learning, with a particular focus on temporal kernels. 3. We present a broad empirical evaluation on visual control tasks and measure the ability of different kernels to recover physical quantities and drive planning performance. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

World models learn how environments respond to actions, enabling agents to plan and learn from predicted outcomes (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018). Generative approaches predict future observations, often by modeling dynamics in a latent space and decoding predictions into images. PlaNet uses learned dynamics to evaluate candidate action sequences, while the Dreamer series trains policies on imagined trajectories (Hafner et al., 2019; 2025). More broadly, generative models support interac-tive environments and spatial world generation (Parker-Holder & Fruchter, 2025; Team HY-World, 2026; World Labs Team, 2026), with world-action models such as Cosmos Policy, DreamZero, and LingBotVA extending these capabilities to action generation for robot control (Kim et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). In contrast, reconstruction-free world models focus prediction on representations without requiring them to reproduce observations. DINO-WM predicts features from a pretrained visual encoder (Zhou et al., 2025) while JEPAs such as LeWorldModel learn the representations alongside the dynamics (LeCun, 2022; Maes et al., 2026). 2Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

JEPA as proposed in LeCun (2022) features the central question of what anti-collapse mechanism is used to guide which information is retained and which is abstracted away. In the cases of I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) and V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026), the target encoder is an exponential moving average of the context encoder with a stop-grad, making collapse difficult to attain but not actually a non-optimal solution. LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) solves anti-collapse by enforcing the distribution of latents to be an isotropic Gaussian, which prevents trivial collapse but does not require any particular information to be retained. 

Spectral representation learning is intimately tied to self-supervised learning. HaoChen et al. (2021) interpret contrastive learning as spectral decomposition of an augmentation graph. Balestriero & LeCun (2022) extend this perspective, showing that several objectives including In-foNCE (van den Oord et al., 2019), VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022), and Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) recover classical spectral embedding methods where the graph is defined implicitly using the choice of augmentations. Our spectral alignment objective extends this work by making previously implicit relations between samples the central object of study. We cover additional families of related work in Appendix A.

Kernel design. Often, robotics data is labeled by informative metadata such as proprioception or depth masks. In such cases, that metadata can be used to construct a supervised kernel. We evaluate three such kernels: state, depth, and proprioception. For each, we use the Euclidean distance be-tween the corresponding feature vectors as the teacher distance. The state kernel uses the simulator state si underlying each observation xi as its feature vector. Although this information is generally unavailable in real-world settings, it lets us evaluate a teacher with access to the full physical state. The proprioception and depth kernels use the robot’s proprioceptive state and the flattened depth mask as feature vectors, respectively. When metadata is not available, the ordering of observations in trajectories itself can form an un-supervised kernel. We use a temporal kernel, which relies on absolute difference between indices for observations ordered in a trajectory: d(xi, x j ) = |i − j|. We also evaluate a quasimetric kernel as a close relative to the temporal kernel. This kernel relies on a distance dq learned through quasi-metric reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2023; Wang & Isola, 2022), which estimates temporal distances between observations according to the optimal goal-reaching policy. While this potentially introduces estimation errors, it labels state pairs by the minimum number of steps between states, in-stead of expressing the temporal separations observed in the data, and can thus compensate for poor data quality. Since the student’s Gram matrix is symmetric, we symmetrize the learned quasimetric 

d(xi, x j ) = dq (xi → xj ) + dq (xj → xi).While supervised kernels can describe relationships between all pairs of inputs, i.e., are fully spec-ified , the unsupervised kernels we use only express relationships for pairs of inputs in the same trajectory. In this underspecified setting, cross-trajectory similarities are excluded from the train-ing objective. We also use a different normalization of the spectral loss: we omit tempering and the target-diagonal rescaling in Equation 6. Each clip’s squared error is normalized by the mean squared magnitude of its centered target before averaging across clips. While representations are fixed up 5Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 to an orthogonal transformation in the fully specified case, underspecification allows the model to form its own abstractions (HaoChen & Ma, 2023).

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

Our empirical evaluation revolves around three visual manipulation environments: OGBench Cube and Scene (Park et al., 2025) and CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022). Scene and CALVIN extend the eval-uation beyond the simpler manipulation settings considered in LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026), introducing multiple objects and articulated fixtures whose states must be represented for control. These environments directly test whether the learned representation preserves information about the surrounding scene as well as the robot itself. We primarily compare SpecWM to LeWM (Maes et al., 2026), and we instantiate SpecWM for each of the kernels introduced in Section 4. A generally ob-served phenomenon in world modeling is that predictors tend to generalize poorly to random actions when trained on only expert data (Fu et al., 2021; Kidambi et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020). Motivated by this, we generate failure data for each environment and append it to the canonical datasets; this decision is ablated in Appendix B.3. Our main points of comparison are success rate with CEM planning (Section 6.1) and linear recov-erability of physical quantities from embeddings (Section 6.2). Further, we provide an ablation of various kernels in both fully specified and underspecified settings in Section 6.3. We extend these results with additional ablations in Appendix B. For CALVIN, we augment training clips with tem-porally distant observations and use a multiscale kernel to capture block movements that short clips often miss. Appendix C details this construction, failure data generation, and evaluation settings. 6.1 PLANNING

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We present world modeling through spectral alignment as a framework for designing the spectral methods that have so far been implicit in self-supervised learning. The core idea is to directly specify the pairwise relationships that representations should preserve through a teacher kernel. We explore theoretical connections to fundamental results in spectral manifold learning and, for a specific choice of kernel, to the likelihood of reaching goals expressed in the data distribution. Further, we validate 9Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 the method empirically and analyze its abstraction properties. We find that readily available kernels result in strong planning performance, which is not entirely correlated with probing accuracy. Pur-suing a better understanding of the mechanisms enabling effective planning would be valuable. We view the study of which kernels are most conducive to large-scale self-supervised training as the next primary direction of work. ## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Kernel alignment , the normalized Frobenius inner product between a candidate kernel and a tar-get similarity matrix, was introduced by Cristianini et al. (2001) as a criterion for kernel selection. Kampffmeyer et al. (2018) later introduced alignment of an autoencoder’s embedding Gram matrix to a provided kernel. We extend this paradigm by removing the reconstruction term and use action conditioned prediction as an additional shaping force for the latent. Their unsupervised kernel de-rives similarity from multiscale clustering of input features, which need not reflect the dynamical relationships relevant to control. We instead study both supervised and unsupervised kernels with a more direct relevance for control and world modeling. Furthermore, our trajectory-local formulation leaves cross-trajectory relationships unspecified, allowing flexibility in the learned geometry. 

Self-Supervised Representations in RL have been explored both as auxiliary objectives in model-free reinforcement learning (Schwarzer et al., 2021), and as part of latent model-based ap-proaches (Schrittwieser et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2024). A further line of work has focused on representation learning as a central component of practical methods, for instance enabling general-ization in behavior cloning (Lawson et al., 2026), or robustness in value-based learning (Zhang et al., 2021). Self-supervised representations are also a core mechanism in unsupervised (Laskin et al., 2021) and zero-shot reinforcement learning (Touati et al., 2023). In particular, contrastive RL (Ey-senbach et al., 2022), forward-backward representation (Touati & Ollivier, 2021; Zheng et al., 2026), ICVF (Ghosh et al., 2023) and TD-JEPA (Bagatella et al., 2026) recover representations which can be connected to the successor measure; we highlight a similar connection for spectral alignment with a temporal kernel. Finally, this class of kernels shares the motivation of early works on temporal distance regression (Tian et al., 2021), which however do not extract representations explicitly.

To measure to what extent the embeddings contain control-relevant objects, we perform linear prob-ing of position coordinates. We sample 5,000 frames from the validation episodes, which the encoder never trained on, and split full episodes into training and testing sets of 80% and 20% respectively, repeated over five splits. Linear readouts use ridge regression, with all preprocessing and regu-larization selection performed using training episodes only. Hyperparameters are selected using episode-grouped subsets of the training split. Ridge coefficients are selected from a logarithmic grid of 13 values between 10 −2 and 10 4. We then report held-out R2 of the best ridge coefficient, using the full 80% to fit the linear probe, averaged over the five splits.
