# ABSTRACT 

Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn world models directly in latent space, but unconstrained autoregressive predictors may contract the la-tent distribution over long rollouts. We introduce Symplectic Hamiltonian JEPA (LeHami ), which replaces the standard predictor with an action-conditioned Hamiltonian flow over a structured latent state (q, p) integrated using an ex-actly symplectic leapfrog step. This construction preserves phase-space volume at every rollout step and rules out uniform contraction, asymptotically stable fixed points, and merging of distinct latent states, independently of the learned param-eters or action sequence. We obtain the momentum-like component from tem-poral derivatives of the learned position-like representation, providing an explicit second-order state for prediction and control. On conservative and actuated vi-sual pendulum environments, LeHami substantially improves long-horizon veloc-ity prediction over an unconstrained JEPA world model and empirically maintains constant latent volume where the baseline contracts. The learned flow also sup-ports accurate integration at unseen sub-frame step sizes and numerical time re-versal. These results show how geometric structure can provide inference-time guarantees that are complementary to representation regularization in latent world models. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) (LeCun et al., 2022) has emerged as a promising framework for action-conditioned latent world modeling. While existing JEPA formulations suc-cessfully prevent encoder collapse: mapping distinct inputs to constant representations, via training-time regularization (Bardes et al., 2021; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) or frozen backbones (Assran et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), maintaining consistent dynamics over extended evaluation rollouts remains a persistent challenge (Maes et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026). Long-horizon stability is further hindered by predictor contraction : in an autoregressive rollout each prediction is fed back as the next input, so even a slight per-step contraction of the transition map compounds over the horizon and progressively shrinks the latent distribution. Physics avoids this contraction through geometric structure . Hamiltonian mechanics governs dy-namics via a single scalar H, constraining conservative trajectories to energy level sets and pre-serving phase-space volume at every discrete step (Arnold, 1989). Crucially, volume preservation persists under actuation, provided the action enters the Hamiltonian. Port-Hamiltonian extensions further cover dissipative systems (Marsden et al., 1999; Zhong et al., 2020; Duong & Atanasov, 2021), making it a physically grounded prior for latent spaces rather than an idealization. We propose LeHami (Symplectic Hamiltonian JEPA), a framework that embeds the latent represen-tation space onto a symplectic manifold where evolution is driven by a learnable action-conditioned Hamiltonian flow Hϕ(q, p, u) over a canonical latent pair (q, p), where u denotes the control action vector, integrated by an exact symplectic leapfrog step (Hairer et al., 2003) and trained end-to-end without decoders or supervised coordinates. LeHami inherits three structural properties from the Hamiltonian flow, illustrated in Fig. 1 (right): (i) Exact volume preservation: Symplectic integration preserves phase-space volume by con-struction, provably eliminating some representation collapse modes during evaluation rollouts for arbitrary action sequences (Proposition 1). (ii) Continuous-time step size: Because Hϕ parameterizes a continuous vector field, a single trained model can be integrated at arbitrary step sizes ∆t without retraining, facilitating train-ing on multi-frequency data (Doshi et al., 2024) and non-uniform planning grids (Tan et al., 2016). (iii) Time-Reversibility: Flipping time ( t → − t) allows backward-in-time rollouts, serving as an essential primitive for computing backward reachable tubes and online safety guarantees in optimal control (Mitchell et al., 2005; Herbert et al., 2017). Moreover, a central objective in physical world modeling is learning representations that capture the 

complete physical state of an environment, especially for control tasks like object interception (Kim et al., 2014) and precision throwing (Zeng et al., 2020). In physics, uniquely determining future evo-lution requires canonical momentum alongside position within a structured phase space. Without this geometric prior, standard JEPA architectures with unstructured predictors resort to architectural design, such as expanding the encoder’s temporal window or context-conditioned predictors to cap-ture velocity, lacking guarantees. In contrast, LeHami leverages this prior to model the latent space as a canonical phase space, enabling complete physical representations. 2Contributions. We summarize our key contributions as follows: • Symplectic latent world model (LeHami): We introduce an end-to-end trainable JEPA framework equipped with a learned separable Hamiltonian predictor over a canonical latent split (q, p). We prove that its volume-preserving step eliminates three specific collapse modes during evaluation rollouts across arbitrary horizons and actions (Proposition 1). • Continuous-time & time reversibility: We show that a single trained predictor Hϕ acts as a continuous vector field, enabling sub-frame time integration without performance degra-dation and achieving numerical time-reversal with round-trip error below 10 −4% without architectural modifications. • Complete physics representation & principled planning: By construction rather than by architectural tuning, LeHami represents the full state through exact geometric struc-ture. Across conservative and actuated pendulum environments, we empirically show that LeHami accurately recovers full system dynamics. This paves the way towards compute-efficient planning to goals that specify the full physical state: position and velocity. 

# 2 LEHAMI : S YMPLECTIC HAMILTONIAN JEPA 

LeHami makes the latent dynamics of a JEPA world model Hamiltonian. Its latent state is a canon-ical pair (qt, pt): the encoder outputs the position-like qt, and the momentum-like pt is its time derivative, obtained by zero-parameter numerical differentiation of the encoder trajectory. The pre-dictor advances this pair by one leapfrog step of a learned, action-conditioned Hamiltonian, so every step of the rollout is a symplectic map. We first state what this structure guarantees (Proposition 1), then give the flow, the multi-step objective and the design choices. A symplectic map preserves the canonical two-form Ω = P 

> i

dq i ∧ dp i, the oriented area spanned in each position–momentum plane (Appendix B), and with it phase-space volume: a cloud of la-tent states can be stretched, rotated and sheared by the rollout, but never squeezed. Constraining the predictor to be such a map therefore rules out, at every step, the contraction available to an unconstrained rollout (Proposition 1). 

Proposition 1 (Symplectic volume preservation and the rollout failure modes it removes ). Let 

Ψh be the leapfrog step of Eq. 4 for step size h generated by a separable Hϕ = Kϕ1 (p) + Vϕ2 (q, u)

with u held fixed within the step. Then Ψh is an exactly symplectic diffeomorphism of R2d for every step size h and every parameter ϕ; it then preserves phase-space volume exactly at every horizon. Here h is the integrator’s step, which equals the observation interval ∆t during training but need not at inference (Section 3.3). Consequently, three collapse modes are ruled out: uniform contraction, convergence to an asymptotically stable fixed point, and the merging of distinct states. They are stated and proved in Appendix C.1. 

Crucially, symplecticity in the latent representation space is a structural guarantee as it holds at ev-ery inference step by construction, not only in expectation over training batches. By endowing the rollout with symplectic geometry, it may also improve long-horizon consistency and planning per-formance. Moreover, it has certain properties, as discussed in Section 1, that are of interest for many applications and that no unstructured predictor can have. We chose the Hamiltonian framework rather than the Lagrangian one; the reasons are given in Appendix C.3. 

Scope and limitations of the guarantee. Three immediate remarks follow Proposition 1. We state them here in short form; Appendix C.2 gives the arguments in full. (i) Shape, not size. Volume preservation forbids the degenerate failures but it does not bound the growth of rollout error. (ii) Regularization is not made redundant. Symplecticity preserves volume, not distributional shape. The two act at different places: The regularizer acts on the encoder distribution in expectation over batches, while symplecticity on every inference step. (iii) Geometric prior, not physical modeling. 

Symplecticity acts purely as an inductive bias on the latent space rather than a physical simulator (Greydanus et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2019; Toth et al., 2019). 2.1 THE ARCHITECTURE

Design choices. The shape of Hϕ. While the core LeHami framework is general and agnostic to the choice of Hamiltonian, in this work we adopt a separable structure. This design choice enables fast, explicit leapfrog integration without requiring iterative numerical solvers. The guarantee is not specific to separable H: for a non-separable Hamiltonian, e.g., exact symplecticity is retained by replacing leapfrog with a non-separable symplectic integrator (Tao, 2016). How p is obtained. 

We enforce the canonical relation p = ˙ q by computing p as the temporal derivative of generalized coordinates q via a fixed Savitzky–Golay (SG) stencil (Gorry, 1990) on the encoder trajectory. This is a least-squares quadratic fit over q that is then differentiated at time step t. The stencil is linear with constant coefficients, so it adds no learned parameters and cannot itself collapse. More details in Appendix C.4. 2.2 RELATED WORK 

Recent work imposes Hamiltonian structure on a learned latent so that predictions remain physically meaningful and useful for action. Cui & Ma (2026) propose Hamiltonian World Models, a perspec-tive that decodes predicted latent trajectories into future observations and plans over the resulting rollouts; as a position paper, it reports no experiments. Tang et al. (2026) decompose the latent into a canonical and a context subspace and train it with a JEPA-style objective, adding the action as an external control term. Luan & Shi (2026) use port-Hamiltonian dynamics as a structural regular-izer alongside the recurrent transition of a decoder-based Dreamer-style agent (Hafner et al., 2019; 2020). Closest to our setting, Zoabi et al. (2026) propose H-JEPA, a dissipative port-Hamiltonian predictor for JEPA world models in which the action enters through an orthonormal port and a port-inverse consistency term infers the executed action from the observed displacement. However, it advances only a fixed slice of a wider code, using a backward difference of that state as context, and its explicit Euler step is not symplectic. LeHami differs in that the action enters the Hamiltonian rather than an external control term, the latent is a canonical phase space (q, p), and every rollout step is exactly symplectic, so phase-space volume is preserved by construction, learned end to end from pixels. 

# 3 EMPIRICAL VALIDATION OF LEHAMI 

We evaluate our approach on two pendulum environments (Fig. 3) rendered as 64 × 64 RGB images. In the conservative pendulum there is no actuation, and episodes vary across three rod lengths. In the actuated pendulum, a recorded sequence of scalar torques (ut)t, drawn uniformly, does work on the system, so energy is no longer conserved; episodes vary across the same three rod lengths and three masses, since under actuation the mass sets the response to the torque. In both environments the true dynamics preserve phase-space volume, which is the property LeHami builds in; the actuated pendulum tests it once energy conservation no longer holds. Methodology. LeHami is designed as a drop-in replacement for the predictor of a JEPA world model, so we compare against LeWorldModel (LeWM) (Maes et al., 2026b), the state-of-the-art among JEPA world models trained end-to-end from pixels, which reported gains over PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025) and DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024). LeWM’s predictor is unstructured: a causal trans-former that takes three context embeddings to predict the next one. LeHami is encoder-agnostic and it replaces only this predictor, plus the fixed SG stencil. The main text uses a temporal ConvNet (Tran et al., 2018) (Appendix Fig. 6) and Appendix E.2 repeats the comparison with a tiny ViT (Wu et al., 2022). Moreover, LeHami is plugged on top of a frozen pre-trained encoder with DINOv2 features (Oquab et al., 2023), yielding DINO-LeHami. We do not compare against reward-driven agents such as Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2019) or TD-MPC (Hansen et al., 2022) variants, since they require task rewards whereas our setting, like LeWM’s, is reward-free, goal-conditioned planning. More details on methodology in Appendix D.1. 

Probing. The pendulum’s state is fully described by the angle θ and the angular velocity ω, and we score their prediction along the rollout by MAE against ground truth; for θ we use MAE rather than 

R2, whose normalising variance is undefined on the circle. Three probe tiers of increasing capacity read the latent: a ridge, a k-NN ( k = 25 ) and a two-layer MLP. Each is fitted once, either on the encoder’s own features (the anchor, K=0) or on the model’s one-step output (K=1), and applied frozen. We report the results in the test split of episodes. The k-NN fitted at K=1 puts the models on equal footing because LeWM’s encoder is per-frame, and only its predictor sees more than one time step.

As discussed, LeHami is a world model that runs at any rate, and in either direction, because the Hamiltonian predictor Hϕ is a continuous-time vector field. We showcase this capability by holding the learned Hϕ fixed and integrating it at s∆t, taking 1/s sub-steps per observed frame, and compare against a Runge–Kutta integration of the true ODE from the same initial condition. Refining below the observation rate leaves the predicted trajectory invariant (Fig. 5 (left)); coarsening breaks it, as an explicit symplectic integrator leaves its stability band. The same property makes the rollout reversible. As shown in Table 2, the baseline LeWM with ConvNet without time window (W=1) fails in the velocity clause because its per-frame goal embedding carries no velocity, even though its predic-tor infers it from context. Expanding the window with a symmetric (tied) kernel only captures speed while remaining direction-blind since it maps ±ω to the same latent (Appendix Table 9). As 92% of the goals require the correct sign (Appendix Fig. 16), it performs below floor on the sign-resolved criterion of Appendix Table 10. In contrast, expanding with untied weights breaks temporal parity, allowing the model to capture signed velocity and achieve the highest success rate. However, this unrestricted temporal formulation incurs a high computational cost per solve. To remain under the 15 TFLOP budget, LeWM W=3 (untied) is forced into larger replanning intervals. In contrast, LeHami structurally guarantees the representation of signed velocity by embedding the system’s physics. Momentum-like embedding is read off the encoder trajectory by a zero-parameter SG stencil, and the Hamiltonian flow itself has 2.23 M parameters, against LeWM’s transformer and its predictor projection with 3.31 M parameters (Appendix Table 5). This computational efficiency allows LeHami to evaluate more trajectories and replan more frequently (R=6 steps with 5 solves) on the velocity and joint clauses, consuming less TFLOPs. While LeWM W=3 (untied) achieves a higher success rate, LeHami provides a structurally rigorous alternative. Replacing the fixed SG stencil with a jointly learned p embedding offers a clear path to closing this performance gap while maintaining the volume-preserving guarantees. 

# 4 CONCLUSION 

We introduced LeHami, a world model architecture that rigorously preserves the geometric proper-ties of second-order physical systems. Its exact symplectic step provides structural guarantees: exact volume preservation that provably eliminates some rollout collapse modes and continuous-time in-tegration with time-reversal. We have shown empirically on the conservative pendulum that LeHami preserves latent volume along the rollout, whereas LeWM contracts it steadily, losing log-volume linearly with the horizon. Consistent with this, LeHami predicts velocity better throughout and angle better at long horizons. With actuation, LeWM reaches the goal angle more often, but it captures signed velocity only when its temporal window is explicitly untied. LeHami instead captures the full state, angle and signed velocity alike, by construction, paving the way towards velocity-constrained planning and FLOP-efficient replanning. 9Limitations and future work. First, while our latent architecture maintains exact volume preser-vation under actuation, strongly dissipative physical systems (e.g., heavy friction) exhibit phase-space volume contraction. Thus, extending LeHami to these regimes e.g., via port-Hamiltonian ex-tensions (Appendix C.4) is a natural direction for future work. Second, LeHami specifically targets velocity-aware tasks, whereas purely static pose tasks are served well by spatial-focused, uncon-strained predictors. Finally, replacing our zero-overhead SG stencil with a learned momentum-like embedding offers a clear path to potentially improve performance without sacrificing long-horizon phase-space stability. Remark 3 (Shape, not size) . A cloud that cannot be squeezed can still be sheared and stretched arbitrarily, so symplecticity places no bound on the growth of rollout error and does not make the rollout accurate. What it removes is the degenerate failure in its three forms: the rollout that shrinks in every direction at once, the one that settles onto an attractor and stops moving, and the one that merges states which started apart. An unconstrained predictor Fθ is under no such constraint, and the one-step objective pushes it the other way. Under any observation or latent noise, the minimiser of E∥Fθ (zt) − zt+1 ∥2 is the conditional mean E[zt+1 | zt], which averages away whatever the anchor leaves undetermined: it contracts volume, and it sends latents that differ only in the averaged-out directions to a common image. Volume loss and merging are therefore the trained optimum for such a map and iterating it drives the rollout onto exactly the attracting set the proposition forbids for Ψh. The proposition says the Hamiltonian rollout cannot enter that regime at any step size h, any parameter ϕ or any horizon. 

Remark 4 (Regularization is not made redundant) . Proposition 1 states that the flow carries phase-space volume exactly and its does not state nothing about the shape of the distribution that volume belongs to. Due to this, regularization techniques are not made redundant. In this work, we leverage the SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) as regularizer, as it is the one used in LeWM and has shown state-of-the-art performance (Maes et al., 2026b). For the isotropic Gaussian N (0, I) installed by SIGReg at the encoder to be invariant under the flow, the radius ∥(q, p)∥2 would itself have to be a conserved quantity of the generator, that is {∥ (q, p)∥2, H ϕ} = 0 . This is a genuine restriction: it is a functional constraint on Hϕ, not a consequence of symplecticity, and a general learned Hamiltonian, which is action-conditioned, and parameterised by free-form networks as we do here, has no reason to satisfy it. The two mechanisms are therefore complementary rather than substitutes. SIGReg shapes the en-coder distribution, during training and in expectation over batches, which is where a collapse of the representation would originate. Symplecticity carries the volume of whatever distribution it is handed, at every inference step and for every input, which is where an iterated map would otherwise contract or explode. 

Remark 5 (Geometric prior, no physical modeling) . We are not modeling the physics of the ob-served system. Hϕ is never fit to a known Hamiltonian and is not claimed to recover one: the true equations of motion enter only as evaluation targets for frozen read-out probes, never as a training signal. This is the point of departure from Hamiltonian neural networks (Greydanus et al., 2019) and SRNN (Chen et al., 2019), where the learned Hϕ is a surrogate for the physical Hamiltonian and recovering it is the goal. The prior models the latent representation space. Writing F for the maps a JEPA predictor may re-alize, we restrict it to the symplectomorphisms reachable as one leapfrog step of a learned separable generator Hϕ(qt, pt, ut); the content of that restriction is geometric (Proposition 1), and it governs the dynamics, or evolution, latent space. The canonical split is structural in the same sense: the encoder emits st = ( qt, pt) with qt, pt ∈ Rd and the flow operates on that split by construction, but qt is not assumed to encode position, nor pt momentum, in any observation-space sense. 15 C.3 WHY HAMILTONIAN AND NOT LAGRANGIAN 

The two formalisms describe the same mechanics in different coordinates, so the choice is made on what each buys a predictor, and two things separate them here. First, the guarantee we rely on is a statement about phase space: symplecticity, and the volume preservation it implies (Proposition 1), are properties of a flow on (q, p) and have no equally cheap counterpart on the tangent bundle 

(q, ˙q) where a Lagrangian lives. Second, the Euler–Lagrange equations are second order in q while Hamilton’s equations are first order on phase space and a separable Hϕ makes the leapfrog step explicit, which is what keeps the map exactly symplectic at any h rather than approximately so. The encode and the SG stencil. Our proposal to model the dynamics in the latent representation space with a Hamiltonian flow is independent of the encoder architecture. However, in the self-supervised setting, the embeddings are being learned, together with its Hamiltonian evolution. For this reason, the encoder has to be sufficiently expressive to encode canonical coordinates. Encoding 

(q, p) is not just separating the embeddings as (z1, z2) = z, in which we just split the embedding into two. We enforce q and p to be canonical by enforcing that the momentum-like coordinates are the time derivative of the position-like coordinates ( p ≡ ˙q). This is a common assumption in classical me-16 chanics, where the momentum is defined as the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to the velocity. We can enforce this by making the encoder produce the momentum-like coordinates as the differentiation of the position-like coordinates. Specifically, we can do this with numerical methods. In this work, we use a Savitzky–Golay filter (Gorry, 1990) to first approximate the position-like coordinates with a polynomial and then compute the momentum-like coordinates as the derivative. Concretely, pt is a fixed linear functional of the encoder trajectory around frame t. We fit a quadratic in time to the encoded positions in a window by least squares whose coefficients are fixed in closed form by the window offsets and polynomial order alone. The least-squares fit sees the encoder values only as its right-hand side, never in its design matrix. Then, its derivative is evaluated at 

t, which for a fixed window is a constant stencil. Three properties of this choice matter. The estimate is at frame t rather than half a step behind it, unlike a backward difference, so qt and pt

refer to the same instant and the pair is canonical in the only sense we can enforce. The stencil is linear in the encoder output with constant coefficients (i.e., no training involved), so it adds no parameters and cannot itself collapse: whatever the momentum block carries, it carries because the encoder trajectory moves. And a least-squares fit over several frames attenuates the high-frequency amplification that differentiation applies to a noisy signal, which a two-point difference does not, at the price of a truncation bias. At the rollout anchor, in training and at inference alike, we use a four-frame causal window, since a centred window would read the frames the model is about to predict; the flow then evolves with no further stencil. The momentum targets used in training are computed with a five-frame centred window, t − 2, . . . , t + 2 , on the encoded q’s. The causality constraint does not apply to the targets: they are read off the encoder trajectory only to be compared with the rollout, never fed into it, so frames on both sides of t are available and using them leaks nothing. Given that choice, the centred window is the better estimator on both counts of the bias–variance trade-off above. Its weights,

The Hamiltonian flow. It a separable Hamiltonian Hϕ(q, p, u) = Kϕ1 (p) + Vϕ2 (q, u) with two independent three-layer tanh MLPs of width 1024, integrated by one exactly symplectic kick–drift– kick leapfrog step per frame. For L EWM the projector output is a single zt ∈ R64 and the pre-dictor is the baseline’s causal autoregressive transformer with a sliding context of three latents. For LeHami, the Hamiltonian autograd path is pinned to fp32. 

The encoder. We implemented both a tiny ViT (Wu et al., 2022) and a temporal ConvNet (Tran et al., 2018). For this temporal encoder, when there is no time window (W=1) it means that the encoder takes one pixel frame xt and produces its respective embedding qt. Then, with these embeddings 

{qt−3, qt−2, qt−1, qt corresponding to multiple time steps, the pt is estimated. We also consider the temporal ConvNet with a temporal window in which pixel frames corresponding to multiple time steps are encoded to produce the embedding corresponding to a single time step. The general form of this architecture embedded into the LeHami framework is shown in Fig. 6. The encoder is a (2+1)D network, meaning the spatiotemporal convolution is factored into two spatial dimensions and one temporal one instead of a joint 3D kernel: a per-frame convolutional stem, one temporal mix, then the unchanged spatial trunk. This encoder implementation allows to train across recording rates. The step size is an argument to the Hamiltonian flow rather than a property of its weights, so one learned Hϕ can in principle be trained on episodes recorded at different rates. We describe the mechanism here for completeness, but no experiment in this paper uses it: every model is trained and evaluated at a single rate, and the conservative dataset is restricted to its ∆t = 0 .1 s slice for exactly this reason (Table 3). We leave the mixed-rate comparison to future work. A dataset that mixes recording rates stores each episode’s frame spacing in a dt frame col-umn; write ∆tep for an episode’s value and ∆tref for a single rate fixed once at config time (model.dt ref ). The leapfrog step carries a ∆t2 factor, so a Vϕ(q) fitted at one rate is wrong by (∆ t/ ∆t′)2 at another: training directly on mixed-rate episodes would hand Kϕ, V ϕ a target that shifts with which episode a clip happens to come from. Dividing by ∆tref removes this factor, so one learned Hϕ stays valid across every rate. We therefore integrate not in ∆tep but in the dimensionless ratio h = ∆ tep / ∆tref 

Within an episode there is a single frame spacing and advancing one frame is one step. As LeHami parametrizes the time step inside the Hamiltonian flow, allowing to input, in principle, any arbitrary time step, episodes recorded at different rates could be trained on together. To do so, a recording rate is set as the reference, ∆tref , and let the integrator step be that episode’s spacing measured in units of it. An episode recorded at the reference rate is integrated with h = 1 , one recorded at twice the spacing with h = 2 , and so on. The learned Hϕ is then the same object for every episode, expressed in reference time, and all the rate information the model needs travels with the clip as a single scalar. This is available to a Hamiltonian predictor precisely because its step size is an argument; an unconstrained predictor has no slot to put h in, so the asymmetry is structural. We do not report a mixed-rate head-to-head, because we did not run one. The two stencils estimate the same derivative to the same order but differ in time parity. The centred weights are antisymmetric in the offset, so reversing the frame order sends pt to −pt exactly. The causal stencil has no such symmetry: under reversal it becomes a forward stencil rather than minus itself. The reversal diagnostic starts from an anchor whose p comes from the causal stencil, which is one reason it is reported as approximate rather than exact, even for a model whose Kϕ were perfectly even. E.1 LEHAMI REPLACES MANUAL DESIGN CHOICES WITH STRUCTURAL PRIORS AND LOWER COMPUTE 

Given a sequence of pixel frames, our goal is to train a world model that captures the system’s physics, specifically its higher-order dynamic states from position to its time derivatives, the velocity and the momentum it determines. In JEPA architectures, capturing these spatiotemporal differences efficiently is a central design consideration. The default implementation of LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b) employs a single-frame encoder (W=1) and delegates the task of learning representations for higher-order moments entirely to a predictor that receives a three-step frame history. A direct limitation of encoding frames individually is the inability to represent goals containing velocity clauses at the single-latent level. 24 Figure 8: The LeHami embeddings on the actuated pendulum are canonical. LeWM has no partitioned momentum block and is reported as a single joint latent z.We assess these different design choices empirically. Fig. 8 shows the R2 of the angle θ and angular velocity ω decoded from anchor latents ( K = 0 ) using a k-NN probe. As expected, LeWM with a single-frame encoder (W=1) fails to capture velocity, yielding an R2 below zero. However, sim-ply expanding the encoder to operate over a time window (W=3) does not inherently improve the representation of velocity and can actually deteriorate it if executed without architectural care. Specifically, a temporal ConvNet with W =3 employs a symmetric kernel by construction when weights are tied (e.g., [a, b, a ] where outer taps share parameters). Consequently, the tied encoder is invariant to reversing the temporal order of the three frames in its window. Reversing time leaves the central angle unchanged while flipping ω → − ω; the states (θ, +ω) and (θ, −ω) are therefore mapped to the exact same latent representation, rendering the sign of the velocity unrecoverable by any probe (see Appendix E.3 for a detailed breakdown). Only even functions of velocity survive: absolute velocity |ω| remains highly decodable ( R2 = 0 .92 ), while signed velocity ω sits at the predict-the-mean floor. While the parity of the kernel can be manually broken—either by untying parameters or feeding explicit frame increments (xt, x t − xt−1)—nothing in the standard JEPA objective forces the model to retain directional information downstream. The encoder is trained merely to make future embed-dings predictable, and a representation blind to the direction of motion predicts its own sign-blind future embeddings perfectly well. The architectural choice is therefore made implicitly, with its failure modes appearing only when evaluating single-latent state readouts. Our proposed LeHami provides a structural solution rather than an empirical workaround. Instead of requiring the temporal encoder to learn velocity implicitly, LeHami partitions the representation into canonical pairs (q, p) and extracts momentum via p = SG( q), an explicitly odd stencil across latents whose parity is fixed by construction. There is no kernel to tune; the Hamiltonian latent structure dictates where ω resides, and encoder parity follows naturally. As shown in Fig. 8, the signal for predicting angular velocity ω originates almost entirely from the momentum block p. Conversely, the angle θ is predominantly captured by the position block q,with minor signal leakage into p arising because p is constructed as a temporal finite difference of

Capturing velocity through the encoder and unstructured predictor.
