Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs), often built upon pretrained video-generation world models,
have emerged as a promising framework for general-purpose and open-ended robot control.
However, their real-world deployment often suffers from discontinuous and jerky execution.
Existing methods typically address this issue by either improving inference efficiency or enhancing
action continuity across prediction chunks, without jointly addressing both challenges. To bridge
this gap, we propose StreamingWAM, a streaming-diffusion framework for WAM-based robot
control. StreamingWAM combines a noise ladder for chunk-wise action prediction, a prefix rollout
module for cross-chunk continuity, and dense video-history memory for temporally consistent
conditioning. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world robotic
tasks demonstrate that StreamingWAM improves action continuity across static and dynamic
settings, achieving competitive performance while reducing steady-state replanning latency to
72.9 ms in simulation and 57.3 ms on the real robot. The method also produces smoother action
transitions, while the streaming staircase reduces sensitivity to the execution horizon K.

Introduction
Recent WAMs are built upon world models, particularly pretrained video-generation models (Ye et al.,
2026b; Li et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). Through pretraining objectives based
on future-state prediction, these models acquire knowledge of environment dynamics, which is then
adapted to robotic domains via fine-tuning for general-purpose and open-ended robot control (Zhang
et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). By leveraging this learned dynamics prior, WAMs can exhibit strong
generalization when deployed on real-world robots across diverse tasks and environments (Wang
et al., 2026b).
Despite their impressive performance, WAMs often exhibit temporally discontinuous and jerky
motions during closed-loop execution (MotuBrain Team, 2026; Black et al., 2025). This behavior is
closely tied to their non-streaming inference procedure: at each replan, existing WAMs regenerate a
complete action chunk from scratch through multi-step denoising and discard the unexecuted suffix
of the previous plan. This procedure creates two coupled limitations. First, the combination of
a large video-generation backbone, joint video–action processing, and iterative denoising makes
WAM inference computationally expensive. Regenerating a complete action chunk at every replan
further compounds this cost, resulting in substantial response latency and slower adaptation to new
observations (Lu et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). Second, independently
generated chunks may be inconsistent with previously executed actions, causing abrupt transitions at
replanning boundaries (Lu et al., 2026; Høeg et al., 2024). Existing methods typically improve either
inference efficiency or cross-chunk action continuity, but rarely both within a unified framework.
To bridge this gap, we propose StreamingWAM (Figure 1), which introduces a streaming diffusion
paradigm into WAMs. Specifically, the noise ladder staggers future action blocks across noise levels
in a persistent buffer, enabling low-latency, single-step steady-state replanning. The prefix rollout
module conditions future-action denoising on a clean prefix of recently executed actions, improving

• We introduce StreamingWAM, which integrates a noise ladder for chunk-wise streaming

Efficient world action models
Recent work accelerates WAM inference through lightweight action
modules, reduced video-side computation, asynchronous video–action denoising, and action-centered
architectures (Yuan et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Guo et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026a).
These methods mainly reduce per-step computation while retaining the standard full-chunk, multi-
step replanning loop; in contrast, StreamingWAM carries and refines a persistent action buffer with a
single forward pass per replan.
Diffusion policies and streaming diffusion
Diffusion Policy generates action chunks through
iterative denoising (Chi et al., 2023), whereas Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP) and Diffusion Forcing
use position- or token-dependent noise levels to support rolling and flexible-horizon generation (Høeg
et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). StreamingWAM adapts this principle to WAMs, preserving the
video-conditioned dynamics prior while enabling persistent, single-pass replanning.

Method
3.1
Problem formulation
At each planning step t, the robot queries the policy with the current visual observation ot and task
instruction l to obtain an H-step chunk of future actions:
at:t+H−1 = π(ot, l).
(1)
Here, H denotes the future-action prediction horizon. A WAM realizes this policy by jointly denoising
the action chunk and a short future video sequence vt:t+T , where T is the video prediction horizon.
At deployment, an execution horizon K ≤H determines how many predicted actions are applied
before replanning: the robot executes at:t+K−1, discards the unused suffix at+K:t+H−1, and replans
when the next observation becomes available. For block-aligned streaming deployment, we use
execution widths that divide H, ensuring that each roll advances the buffer by an integer number of
complete noise-ladder blocks.

3.2
StreamingWAM
Overview
We propose StreamingWAM (Figure 2), a streaming diffusion formulation built on a
Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) WAM with dedicated video and action experts. It combines a prefix
rollout module that conditions prediction on clean executed actions, a noise ladder that progressively
refines a persistent future-action buffer, and dense video-history memory that supplies recent visual
context. Together, these components amortize denoising across planning calls, reducing steady-state
latency while improving cross-chunk continuity. StreamingWAM requires neither additional trainable
parameters nor additional training data.
Prefix rollout module and dense video-history memory
At each planning step t, we form an
action sequence consisting of a clean prefix of P previously executed actions, followed by H future
action slots. The prefix is not a prediction target: during training, it is populated with ground-truth
actions preceding the target chunk, whereas at deployment, it contains the actions actually executed
by the robot. These prefix actions are provided without noise, allowing the action branch to model
the future action sequence as
pθ(at:t+H−1 | z(ot−m+1:t, l), at−P :t−1) .
(4)
Here, ot−m+1:t forms the dense video-history memory, capturing short-term visual dynamics that
complement the executed-action prefix to improve the temporal consistency of action generation.
As planning advances, newly executed actions are rolled into the conditioning prefix, while the
unexecuted portion of Bt is shifted forward and reused. Thus, overlapping future actions are not
regenerated from scratch, breaking the cross-call independence of standard WAM inference described
in Section 3.1. Following Black et al. (2025), the action prefix also provides longer-term context than
the short visual-history window.

Multi-width staircase training.
The staircase is entirely determined by the block width K, and we
do not hold it fixed. For each training example, we independently sample the execution horizon K
uniformly from a finite candidate set K and construct the corresponding noise profile. We retain the
standard shared-timestep objective for a fraction of training steps to preserve the model’s multi-step
denoising capability, which is required during the warm-up phase at the beginning of each episode,
when the initial plan is generated from pure noise.

Warm-up initialization.
At the beginning of each episode, we perform a one-time warm-up to
construct the initial persistent buffer B0. Specifically, we first generate a clean action chunk using the
standard multi-step flow-matching sampler and then re-noise its blocks according to the staircase,
following the denoise initialization strategy of Høeg et al. (2024). Since no executed-action history
is available at this point, the action prefix apre is initialized with zeros. After this warm-up, each
planning call follows the prefix-rollout procedure and requires only a single joint forward pass.

Metrics.
For most tasks, we report success rate (%); for Whack-a-Mole, we report valid activations
of the currently lit button within a fixed time budget. We distinguish the once-per-episode time to first
action (TTFA), which includes buffer initialization, from the steady-state replanning latency used
during normal closed-loop control (Lu et al., 2026). Continuity metrics include per-step position
change, joint displacement from the initial posture, and a discrete acceleration proxy.

4.3
Real robot experiments
Setup
We deploy on a Franka Research 3 arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, two external Intel
RealSense D435i cameras, and a wrist-mounted ZED Mini following the DROID (Khazatsky et al.,
2024) configuration. The control loop runs at 15 Hz on an RTX 5090 workstation. Demonstrations are
collected via GELLO joint-space teleoperation; details are provided in Section E. On the deployment
GPU, steady-state per-replan latency is 57.3 ms for our model (1 denoise step) and 61.5 ms for
π0.5 (10 steps), both within the 66.7 ms control budget. Fast-WAM and FastWAM-Joint require
182.6 ms and 368.6 ms, respectively, with their standard 10-step inference, missing multiple deadlines
per replan (Table 7). We therefore use 10 steps on static tasks and one step on dynamic tasks for
Fast-WAM and FastWAM-Joint; preliminary 10-step dynamic deployments yielded near-zero success
because the policy could not react within the task time scale. The six evaluation tasks are illustrated
in Figure 5, with detailed descriptions provided in Section E.1.
Results
Figure 6 shows that StreamingWAM performs best overall. It achieves the highest aggregate
success across the five manipulation tasks (168/250, 67.2%, Wilson 95% CI [61.2, 72.7], versus
120/250, 48.0% [41.9, 54.2], for the strongest baseline), leads both dynamic tasks (32/50 on conveyor
handover and 20/50 on car pursuit, compared with baseline maxima of 15/50 and 8/50), and records
the most whack-a-mole presses (424 vs. 408).
Analysis
Two complementary temporal effects may explain the task-level gains. First, the largest
margins occur on the dynamic tasks. A single frame locates an object but generally leaves its velocity
ambiguous, whereas consecutive real observations reveal frame-to-frame motion. This temporal evi-
dence may allow the WAM’s future-state predictor to anticipate object motion before action selection.
At the same time, single-step streaming replanning lowers the steady-state latency, allowing those
predictions to be refreshed at the task’s reaction time scale. The dynamic gains therefore plausibly
reflect the joint effect of motion evidence from real observation history, the WAM’s predictive prior,
and low-latency replanning, rather than any one factor alone. In stage-matched conveyor trials,

(2) Multi-width staircase training improves performance across execution horizons.
As shown
in Table 3, replacing the full training set K with a fixed staircase width of K = 4 preserves low
variation across execution horizons but reduces success at every evaluated value of K, including
its training width (75.31% versus 79.36% at K = 4). The fixed-width model peaks at K = 4, as
expected, but generalizes less effectively to other execution horizons. These results support the design
motivation in Section 3.2: sampling multiple widths exposes the model to more diverse staircase
geometries and improves performance across the entire K-sweep.
(3) Dense visual history improves success rates and reduces variation across execution horizons.
With P and S held fixed, replacing dense visual history (stride 1) with sparse history (stride 4) shifts
the entire K-sweep downward and nearly doubles the Spread (Table 3). The dense frames supply
recent visual context that overlaps with the clean-prefix window, giving the model a more accurate
estimate of the current scene state. The benefit is most pronounced at small K, where frequent
replanning places greater importance on recent observations.
(4) Auxiliary training choices mainly affect accuracy rather than horizon robustness.
Uniform
loss weighting stays within 1.59 pp of the full model across all K and changes Spread only from
6.50 to 7.10 pp, indicating that position-dependent weighting is a modest refinement. On the full
evaluation, removing the warm start reduces the K = 4 result by 5.15 pp (74.21% versus 79.36%).
This shows that initialization mainly raises the initial buffer’s accuracy; the staircase remains the
mechanism tested for robustness across execution horizons.
(5) The streaming advantage vanishes when the execution width spans the full prediction
horizon.
The training noise profiles sample Ktrain = {1, 2, 4, 8}, all block-aligned divisors of
H = 16, while K = 16 is held out from training. At K = H = 16, the horizon becomes one block
and loses the inter-block staggering required for streaming. The full model then drops by 5.87 pp
from K = 8 to 16 (78.73% to 72.86%), while its margin over the temporal-context-only (+P) variant
shrinks from 13.53 pp at K = 4 to 0.37 pp at K = 16. Thus, this boundary stress test shows that the
streaming benefit vanishes when the execution width spans the prediction horizon.
5
Limitations
Our study has two main limitations. First, our experiments use a single base-model family, Fast-WAM.
Although the streaming formulation does not require a specific WAM backbone, its effectiveness
10


on other backbones remains to be validated. Second, our model does not use large-scale embodied
pre-training. On RoboTwin 2.0’s randomized-initialization split, embodied-pretrained policies such
as π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) retain substantially higher success under scene variation.
This gap may partly reflect the broader data coverage and stronger generalization provided by cross-
embodiment pre-training. Combining such pre-training with streaming denoising is an important
direction for future work.
6
Conclusion
We presented StreamingWAM, a streaming-diffusion framework that amortizes action denoising
across planning calls through a persistent rolling buffer. Its streaming staircase progressively refines
future actions, while the executed-action prefix and dense visual history maintain temporal context
across replans. Across LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-robot tasks, StreamingWAM achieves
competitive task success, reduces steady-state replanning latency to 72.9 ms in simulation and
57.3 ms on the real robot, improves cross-chunk action continuity, and compresses execution-horizon
sensitivity from 35.75 to 6.50 percentage points. Its largest real-robot gains appear on moving-target
and multi-stage tasks, consistent with the value of temporal context and responsive replanning.
Together, these results support streaming action generation as a practical approach to more responsive
and continuous WAM control.

trajectories across seven collection tasks: block stacking, open drawer, close drawer, weigh-and-place,
conveyor handover, car pursuit, and whack-a-mole. Open and close drawer are collected separately
but belong to the same manipulation family. Evaluation reports the open-drawer variant and omits
the easier close-drawer variant, yielding the six evaluation tasks described below. The number of
trajectories varies by task, with some tasks containing fewer than 100 demonstrations. Evaluation
uses 50 trials per manipulation task and 50×30 s sessions for whack-a-mole. Within π0.5 and
FastWAM-Joint, Async and RTC variants share a checkpoint and differ only in execution strategy.
Introduced by Lu et al. (2026), TTFA measures the wall-clock time from an episode’s initial ob-
servation to its first executable action at the controller. For StreamingWAM, this once-per-episode
quantity includes the warm start that initializes the persistent action buffer. Normal closed-loop
control does not repeat that cost, so our primary latency metric is steady-state replanning time; the
initialization latency is reported separately. All measurements use the same RTX 5090 GPU with
CUDA-synchronous timing.
During simulation evaluation, StreamingWAM uses synchronous control: the environment waits for
each policy call before executing the returned K-action block. Real-robot deployment instead uses
asynchronous control. For K = 4, the next inference is launched only after the first three actions of
the current block have finished, so inference overlaps with execution of the fourth action.
The π0.5 simulation entries use the public implementation defaults: LIBERO predicts 10 actions
and executes five per query, while RoboTwin predicts and executes 50; both use 10 flow-matching
integration steps. Async and RTC therefore share the same simulation defaults and differ only in the
real-robot execution stack.
The RoboTwin Joint setting executes K = 24 actions, whereas StreamingWAM uses K = 8; the
latter value is an execution setting and must not be confused with the training set Ktrain. The
real-robot FastWAM-Joint RTC row is the configuration reported in Figure 6 and Table 12. The
FastWAM-Joint LIBERO and LIBERO-PLUS result in Table 1 follows its official configuration with
K = 10. StreamingWAM’s steady-state one is a rolling update of a persistent buffer, not one-step
denoising of an independently redrawn Joint chunk.
A.4
Baselines
The LIBERO comparison includes 13 baselines spanning both VLA policies and WAMs, grouped
according to whether they use embodied data during pretraining. Eight methods use embodied-data
pretraining: π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025), GE-ACT (Liao et al., 2025), VLA-JEPA (Sun
et al., 2026), X-VLA (Zheng et al., 2025), OpenVLA-OFT (Kim et al., 2025), π0-Fast (Pertsch
et al., 2025), π0 (Black et al., 2024), and UniVLA (Bu et al., 2025). The remaining five do not:
Faster-WAM (Ma et al., 2026), 4D-WAM (Yang et al., 2026), Fast-WAM-Joint, WorldVLA (Cen
et al., 2025), Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026). The RoboTwin comparison additionally includes AHA-
WAM (Cai et al., 2026). Among these, Fast-WAM-Joint, the jointly trained variant of Fast-WAM,
serves as our closest direct baseline because it shares the same model architecture with Streaming-
WAM while omitting the three modules introduced in Section 3.2.

E
Real-robot experiment details
E.1
Task descriptions
We evaluate six manipulation tasks, grouped by whether the target object moves during execution.
Static tasks: block stacking—stack five layers of blocks; open drawer—grasp the handle and pull
the drawer open; and weigh-and-place—place an apple on a kitchen scale, read the weight, then
transfer it to a bowl. Dynamic tasks: conveyor handover—pick a moving apple off a conveyor belt
and place it into a bowl; car pursuit—intercept an apple riding a curved-path toy car and place it
into a bowl; and whack-a-mole—press the randomly lit button on a response-time toy. The dynamic
tasks require the policy to continuously adapt its actions to moving objects and therefore directly
evaluate reactive control in time-varying environments.
E.2
Observation preprocessing
Figure 8 shows a representative model input frame from the real robot. Each of the three camera
views (two exterior RealSense D435i and one wrist-mounted ZED Mini) is resized to 128×224
and vertically stacked into a 384×224 tensor (all dimensions are H×W). In simulation, LIBERO
provides two views horizontally concatenated into a 224×448 tensor.
