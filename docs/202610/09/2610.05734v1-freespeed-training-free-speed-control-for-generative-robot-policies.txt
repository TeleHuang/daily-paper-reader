Online control of execution speed is essential for deploying robot policies in real-world scenarios, as robots may need to speed up under time constraints or slow down to facilitate human interaction and improve safety. However, imitation-learned policies inherit the execution speed of their demonstrations, and test-time speed modification can introduce unrecoverable out-of-distribution observations, reducing task success. We observe that the directional inconsistency of action chunks reflects task-phase criticality, indicating how aggressively action step lengths can be modified while preserving task success. Based on this observation, we introduce FreeSpeed , a training-free module that post-processes action chunks from pretrained policies. FreeSpeed resamples each predicted chunk at the requested rate, then uses directional inconsistency between adjacent actions as the primary signal for rescaling. This signal adaptively determines how closely the execution speed can approach the requested speed, allowing flexible speed adjustment within the evaluated limits without compromising task success. Across three policy families and 50 simulated tasks, FreeSpeed supports online speed changes, with realized execution rates spanning 0.22 × to 2.53 × among settings that preserve per-task success. Across four real-world manipulation tasks, FreeSpeed achieves an average success rate of 94 .0%, matching the frozen policy’s 93 .8%, while realizing execution rates from 0.38 × to 1.97 ×.

Modulating execution speed online matters when robots operate across changing tasks and deployment conditions. Fast motion improves efficiency in repetitive manipulation (Zeng et al., 2020), whereas slow motion can be essential for safe and comfortable human–robot interaction in shared workspaces (Lachner et al., 2021; Hostettler et al., 2025), particularly as robots are increasingly deployed in diverse human-centered tasks (Fan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026b,c). Within a single manipulation task, a robot may need to move quickly during routine phases, such as object transport, and slow down during phases that require precision, such as object placement and grasping (Guo et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025a; Yuan et al., 2025). A deployed policy should therefore accept speed requests during execution rather than operate at a single pace fixed during training. Imitation-based policies learn the execution speed implicitly from demonstrations (Shi et al., 2026), and they do not normally expose the speed control interface. Despite the impressive speed-modification performance of existing methods (Yuan et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025a; Hu et al., 2026a; Jing et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2025), almost all require a training process closely tied to task-specific data augmentation. This process typically involves specifying speed factors, post-processing collected demonstrations, and then fine-tuning or retraining a policy in a speed-informed manner. This dependence can limit the methods’ adaptability to unseen scenarios, making speed control feasible but difficult to generalize. Therefore, it is necessary to explore training-free, model-independent speed control that can transfer directly across tasks and pretrained models. A straightforward approach is to downsample or upsample the predicted action chunk at test time. At a fixed command frequency, combining or skipping action steps accelerates execution by reducing the number of executable steps, while interpolation slows execution by adding interme-diate steps. Despite its simplicity, this approach changes step lengths, which can induce out-of-distribution (OOD) observations (Yang et al., 2024; Arachchige et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Nam et al., 2025) and cause subsequent trajectory predictions to drift (Ross et al., 2011) (Section 5.5). This is particularly risky during task phases that require precision, such as grasping and placing, where execution errors at critical steps can cause the entire task to fail. To this end, we identify directional inconsistency as a test-time indicator of task-phase criticality. In the studied tasks, inconsistency tends to be high during critical phases and low otherwise. Adjusting action step lengths according to this signal helps preserve task success while allowing more aggressive speed changes during less critical phases. Building on this insight, we introduce FreeSpeed, a training-free, plug-and-play module for frozen action-chunking policies. FreeSpeed also builds on action resampling but adapts the command step length according to the directional consistency of the predicted action chunk. It pushes execution speed toward its limit when consecutive action directions remain consistent and gradually shifts execution back toward the original speed as inconsistency increases. It uses only the policy’s predicted motion and requires no policy updates, additional data, or a learned scheduler. The same interface accepts both fixed and time-varying rate commands. The contributions of this work are as follows. • We use adjacent-action cosine inconsistency as a training-free proxy for phase criticality and show in Section 5.5 that adapting step-length rescaling to this signal keeps the frozen policy’s observations close to the training distribution under both acceleration and deceleration. • We introduce FreeSpeed, a training-free module that combines temporal resampling with continuous inconsistency-dependent action scaling for fixed and time-varying speed control. • We evaluate FreeSpeed on the frozen π0.5, Fast-WAM, and task-specific Flow Matching policies, across 50 LIBERO tasks and four real-robot tasks, under fixed and time-varying commands from 0.2× to 4×. # 2 Related Work 

Existing methods differ in which components are modified to enable speed control. They can be broadly grouped into three approaches: (1) incorporating speed variation into policy learning; (2) adapting both policy learning and the execution framework; and (3) training auxiliary speed-control modules while keeping the base policy frozen. The first group modifies the policy through demonstration retiming (Guo et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025a; Jing et al., 2026), rate-aware objectives or architectures (Hu et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026), or speed-augmented pretraining followed by RL fine-tuning (Nam et al., 2025). These methods primarily target faster execution, with limited attention to deceleration or user control. TempoVLA (Jing et al., 2026) supports user-specified rates but requires training to enable this capability. The second group combines policy adaptation with precise tracking. SAIL and RACE process collected demonstrations, use reached states as action prediction targets, and combine execution retiming with high-gain control (Arachchige et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026). SAIL’s ablation indicates that high-gain control contributes substantially to its acceleration gains. Building on this approach, Yang et al. (2026) further learn faster-than-demonstration execution from human-modulated rollouts. The third group freezes the base policy and trains an external speed controller. SpeedTuning trains a speed policy online via reinforcement learning (Yuan et al., 2025), whereas SuP trains a downsampling scheduler offline using a learned world model (Wu et al., 2026). Both require additional training and select execution rates autonomously rather than follow user-specified rates. In contrast, FreeSpeed adjusts a frozen policy’s output toward rates specified by the user in real time, supporting both acceleration and deceleration. It requires no post-processing of training data, no speed-informed retraining, and no change to the low-level control law or controller gains. We seek to control the execution rate through two mechanisms. First, skipping or repeating predicted actions changes the time required to execute a chunk at a fixed robot control frequency. Second, scaling the translation and rotation increments changes the trajectory length covered by each chunk. Both the resampling and rescaling effects should be adjustable through user input between successive policy inferences to accommodate real-time speed commands. The user specifies the desired execution rate through a command ρt > 0, where ρt = 1 denotes the original rate, ρt > 1 requests faster execution, and ρt < 1 requests slower execution. The same value acts as the stride at which the predicted chunk is resampled. The performance of execution rules is evaluated using task success rate and realized execution rate br, defined as the ratio of the steps used to complete a task under the policy’s original execution to those used after applying the rule. The objective is for br to approach ρt while preserving the policy’s original success rate. 

# 4 FreeSpeed: Inconsistency-Gated Chunk Post-Processing 

At each replan, FreeSpeed resamples the predicted chunk of incremental (delta) actions on a temporal grid with stride ρt and scales the resampled actions by a factor ft. Under this scheme, acceleration skips predicted actions, while deceleration splits each action. The remainder of this section formalizes these operations and introduces the mapping from ρt to ft. Figure 2 visualizes the execution loop. 

4.1 Why Cosine Inconsistency and Its Formulation 

Naively modifying the execution rate of predicted actions alters the distribution of the resulting observations. Intuitively, speeding up makes observations sparser along the executed trajectory, whereas slowing down makes them denser. This sometimes shifts the policy’s inputs away from the training distribution, leading to action-chunk predictions that are poorly supported by the training data. We further investigate and quantify this effect in Section 5.5. The results suggest that the shift in observation distributions can in turn affect action predictions and, consequently, task success. We further observe that chunk geometry is a determining factor in whether the observation distribution shifts under speed modification. Along straight segments, the original and modified chunks produce similar observation distributions. However, when a chunk contains turns or noise, these distributions differ substantially, as further illustrated in Figure 6c. 4.2 Resampling and Rescaling with Cosine Inconsistency 

As discussed above, at a fixed robot control frequency, execution speed can be modified by resampling action chunks represented as incremental (delta) actions. For acceleration, some predicted actions can be skipped with the retained actions rescaled, reducing the number of command steps needed to cover the distance traveled by the original chunk. Alternatively, multiple actions can be summed into a larger step to achieve the same goal (Wu et al., 2026). For deceleration, each original action can be split into multiple sub-actions that preserve its direction while reducing the step length. However, as discussed in Section 4.1, resampling can shift the observation distribution, making subsequently predicted actions less controllable. Such shifts can cause failure during critical task phases, particularly when grasping or releasing an object. To this end, we use cosine inconsistency (CI) to guide rescaling and mitigate the observation distribution shifts caused by naive speed modification. Intuitively, when CI is small, action steps are scaled toward the commanded speedup or slowdown limit, as the resulting distribution shift is less likely to disrupt execution. When CI is large, more conservative scaling is applied, as this may indicate a critical task phase in which distribution shifts can cause failure. # 5 Experiments 

5.1 Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate three policy architectures: VLA, WAM, and task-specific flow matching. We use checkpoints of 

π0.5 (Physical Intelligence, 2025; Black et al., 2024) and Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) that were pretrained on 40 tasks spanning LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long (Liu et al., 2023). We keep these policies frozen and evaluate FreeSpeed using their action predictions. In addition to these released checkpoints, we train task-specific flow-matching policies (Lipman et al., 2023) on 10 LIBERO-90 tasks and 4 real-robot tasks. Figure 3 shows representative simulation and real-robot task setups. 

5.2 Cosine Inconsistency Tracks the Task Phase 

We illustrate how Icos varies with task progress using five LIBERO-90 tasks as examples, each with roughly 50 

demonstrations. For every demonstration, we slide a window of length H along the trajectory and compute 

Icos within each window. Figure 4a plots one curve per task. Segments with high Icos correspond to decisive task phases, identified in these examples as grasping and placement in pick-and-place tasks. This partly reflects how human operators behave during data collection: they slow down and hesitate during critical phases and move faster otherwise (Belkhale et al., 2023). Such behavior can introduce substantial noise and turns into the recorded trajectories. Through training, the policy learns these features and reproduces them in its predicted action chunks. At inference, these features provide an indication of whether the robot is at a decisive moment, how much error that phase can tolerate, and how far the execution rate can be adjusted. 5.3 Evaluation in Simulation 

We compare the following methods. (1) FreeSpeed applies Equation (5). (2) Vanilla resampling is a training-free baseline used in prior works (Wu et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025a). When the policy predicts absolute end-effector poses or joint positions, this baseline simply resamples the actions and sends them to the robot for execution. In our delta-action setting, the equivalent operation sums the skipped action increments into the retained action. (3) w/o CR combines resampling with naive rescaling by the commanded stride, formally ft = ρt, and serves as an ablation of FreeSpeed without CI-based rescaling. All methods use the same frozen checkpoints and task initial states, with the unchanged 

1× policy as the reference. Under deceleration, vanilla resampling and w/o CR are equivalent, so only w/o CR is reported. We run 50 rollouts per simulated task and report success rate, mean completion steps over successful trials, and realized rate. The realized rate is the ratio of the reference’s mean completion steps to those of the evaluated method, computed only over trials in which both succeed. Flow Matching is evaluated at {0.3, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2}× ,and π0.5 and Fast-WAM at {0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4}× . Table 2 reports the Flow Matching results. Across the four non-reference strides, FreeSpeed maintains or exceeds the 1× reference’s mean success rate of 89 %, with realized rates ranging from 0.348 × to 1.431 × across individual tasks. In comparison, both vanilla resampling and w/o CR achieve lower success rates than the 1× reference. Figure 5 extends the comparison to π0.5 and Fast-WAM across the four LIBERO suites, each containing 10 tasks. At a 2× command, FreeSpeed maintains the reference success rates for both policies, whereas vanilla resampling reduces success by 9.2 and 12 .7 percentage points and w/o CR by 8.0 and 12 .3 percentage points, respectively. Under deceleration, FreeSpeed outperforms w/o CR at every slowdown stride on both policies. Overall, across slowdown and speedup commands ranging from 0.2× to 4×, FreeSpeed achieves higher success rates than both baselines at every evaluated stride. Across individual tasks from both policies, the realized rates that preserve each task’s own 1× success rate range from 0.22 × to 2.53 ×.

# 6 Conclusion 

FreeSpeed enables test-time execution-speed control for frozen action-chunking policies. It computes cosine inconsistency between adjacent actions within each predicted chunk and uses this signal to adapt rescaling under fixed or time-varying commands. Evaluations with Flow Matching, π0.5, and Fast-WAM on 10 LIBERO-90 tasks and the four LIBERO suites demonstrate speed adjustment over a range of realized rates while maintaining success rates comparable to the frozen-policy references. Across four real-robot tasks, FreeSpeed likewise matches the frozen policy’s mean success rate when averaged over six commands from 0.2× to 4×.The main limitation of FreeSpeed is that its acceleration ceiling is set by how much free motion a task contains, as detailed in Appendix F. 10 AI Use Statement

B.1 Simulation Tasks 

The flow-matching evaluation uses 10 tasks drawn from LIBERO-90, listed in Table A2. Each task provides 50 human demonstrations, and we train one policy per task. All 10 are pick-and-place tasks, sharing two critical phases: grasping the object and releasing it at the target. These shared phases provide a common basis for examining the effects of speed modification. The tasks differ in their placement constraints, particularly the allowable variation in release position, and we group them into three types accordingly. 

Structure-constrained placement. The target is a fixed part of the scene, such as an open drawer or a cabinet shelf. The release position allows only a few centimetres of deviation, and the approach must clear the surrounding geometry. Overshooting during accelerated execution can cause the object or gripper to collide with nearby structures. We therefore expect these tasks to be particularly sensitive to speed increases during the approach. 

Bounded receptacle placement. The target is a container or a flat region with an edge, such as a basket, a tray or a stovetop, that accepts the object within a limited area. The release position allows several centimetres of variation in the horizontal plane, but the object must still clear the rim or that edge before release. These tasks permit greater variation in placement than the first group but remain sensitive to motion near the boundary and during release. 

Open relational placement. The target is specified relative to another object on an open surface, such as on a plate or to its left. Without enclosing geometry, these tasks allow the greatest placement tolerance among the three groups. We therefore expect placement to be less sensitive to speed modification, while grasping remains a critical phase. This grouping is specific to our analysis and reflects how placement constraints may affect tolerance to speed modification. Table 2 groups the tasks accordingly, allowing the effects of fixed-stride execution to be compared across different levels of placement tolerance.

Since we train the base flow-matching models from scratch, we provide their architecture and training details below. The policy takes eight consecutive observations and predicts twelve seven-dimensional actions. Each observation includes two 128 ×128 RGB images, joint positions, end-effector state, gripper state, and a language instruction. Frozen BERT-base-cased features (Devlin et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2025) are attention-pooled into a 64-dimensional language vector. Each camera uses a separate truncated ResNet-18 encoder (He et al., 2016) with language-conditioned FiLM (Perez et al., 2018; Brohan et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2022; Haldar et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025b) and spatial-softmax pooling (Levine et al., 2016). Each of the six modalities is encoded into 64 dimensions, and the features are concatenated across the observation history to form a 

3072 -dimensional conditioning vector. A conditional one-dimensional U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) with channel widths (256 , 512 , 1024) generates actions. The action-generation branch contains approximately 111 .1

million trainable parameters. Training uses linear Gaussian-to-action paths and a mean-squared-error loss on the predicted vector field. The documented configuration uses batch size 128 and AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 

10 −4, weight decay 10 −4, and cosine learning-rate decay (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) to 10 −5. Inference uses ten explicit Euler steps. For the real-robot checkpoint, generating one action chunk takes 22 .9 ± 0.45 

ms on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 at batch size one using FP32. This timing includes observation encoding but excludes preprocessing, communication, and BERT computation, as BERT features are precomputed and cached. Deployment logs separately report an average server inference time of 33 .2 ms. 

C.2 FreeSpeed 

For each commanded rate ρt, we fix two scaling endpoints, fs(ρt) and fc(ρt). The factor equals fs when cosine inconsistency is zero and approaches fc as inconsistency increases. Under acceleration, fc = 1 , so increasing inconsistency brings the executed step lengths closer to the original predictions while retaining temporal resampling. Under deceleration, fs = ρt, giving the full slowdown scaling for consistent chunks, whereas increasing inconsistency moves the factor toward fc, which is closer to 1. Table A4 lists all endpoint values used in this work. These values depend only on the commanded rate and are shared across tasks, policies, and rollouts, without task-specific tuning. C.3 Controller Action Limits in Simulation 

LIBERO controls the arm through robosuite’s operational space controller (Khatib, 1987; Zhu et al., 2025; Todorov et al., 2012) using end-effector pose increments (Aljalbout et al., 2024). The controller clips each action dimension independently to [−1, 1] and linearly maps it to translation increments of up to 0.05 m or rotation increments of up to 0.5 rad per component. Such element-wise clipping can alter both the magnitude and direction of an action, distorting scaled actions that exceed the bounds. To avoid this distortion, we expand the input range to [−L, L ] and proportionally expand the output range. This preserves the end-effector increment per unit action while relaxing the clipping bound. All other controller settings remain at their LIBERO defaults. Since the largest fs used in this work is 3.6, we set L = 4 for every rate-modified condition and retain the default L = 1 for the 1× reference. All methods use the same L for a given commanded rate. Under the fixed 2× stride, 59 .7% of actions exceed the original input bound ( max j |aj | > 1). With L = 4 ,no actions exceed the expanded bound, with the largest observed absolute action component being 1.52 .In contrast, the 1× and 0.5× conditions are nearly unaffected by the original bound. On the real robot, translation and rotation increments are limited to 35 mm and 60 mrad per step, respectively. No commands exceed these limits at the execution rates evaluated in this work. 

# D Comparison Criteria and Data Sources 

This section defines the criteria used in Table 1 and identifies the sources of the results plotted in Figure 7a. 

D.1 Criteria in the Capability Table 

Each column of Table 1 represents a binary criterion. A filled dot indicates that the capability is demonstrated in the paper; an open dot indicates that it is not demonstrated. 

No rate training. Whether the rate-control mechanism requires no training or fine-tuning. This criterion concerns the rate-control mechanism itself, rather than the entire policy pipeline. RACE satisfies it because its retiming uses classical time-optimal path parameterization (Pham and Pham, 2018), which requires no training. Although RACE retrains the policy to predict reached states rather than commanded states, this changes the policy’s prediction target rather than introducing a learned rate-control mechanism. 

Unmodified demos. Whether the method uses the collected demonstrations without offline modification to support speed control, such as retiming, downsampling, segment annotation, or conversion to a different supervision target. Under this definition, AutoSpeed satisfies the criterion: it retimes the same future trajectory at several rates to generate candidate supervision targets, evaluates them using a composite cost, and trains toward the lowest-cost candidate. These operations occur within the training objective rather than through offline modification of the demonstration dataset. 

Unchanged controller. Whether the reported speed modification retains the low-level control law and controller gains used during demonstration collection. Controller gains and tracking accuracy affect how 18 quickly the robot can follow motion commands. Replacing the original controller with a higher-gain controller can therefore make part of the reported speedup attributable to changes in low-level control. 

User-set rate. Whether the method accepts a user-specified target execution rate and adjusts execution accordingly. A rate inferred or optimized autonomously by the method does not satisfy this criterion. 

Slow-motion. Whether the method supports execution below the original 1× rate. Methods restricted to skipping actions or compressing trajectories in time do not satisfy this criterion unless they also provide a mechanism for slowing execution. 

In-episode rate. Whether the user can update the target rate during an episode and have the new command take effect online within the method’s supported range. This criterion concerns user control rather than internally varying execution rates. SuP’s scheduler changes the downsampling rate between action chunks, but it does not accept a user-specified target rate and therefore does not satisfy this criterion. 

D.2 Method Selection in the Benchmark Comparison 

Figure 7a compares changes in success rate with realized execution rate, both relative to each method’s own 

1× reference. Table 1 shows that existing methods differ from FreeSpeed in their training requirements, demonstration processing, controller settings, support for user-specified rates, deceleration, and rate adjustment within an episode. Since no method matches the complete setting of FreeSpeed, we select representative methods along two complementary dimensions: post-processing action chunks from a frozen policy and accepting user-specified execution rates. We select SuP and TempoVLA to represent these two dimensions. SuP freezes the base policy and downsamples its predicted action chunks, making it the closest comparison in execution mechanism. It trains a scheduler through offline reinforcement learning using counterfactual trajectories generated by a learned world model. The scheduler selects rates autonomously, and SuP applies benchmark-specific gripper compensation through increased control gain or action magnitude. TempoVLA is the closest comparison in control interface: it accepts user-specified rates, supports acceleration and deceleration, and allows rate changes during execution. These capabilities, however, require a speed-conditioned policy trained on demonstrations retimed to multiple rates. SAIL and RACE study execution beyond demonstration speed while addressing the associated tracking challenges. Their evaluations use high-gain controllers that differ from those used during demonstration collection, so their reported results reflect both rate modulation and changes in low-level control. The contribution of the controller is evident in SAIL’s ablation: without high-gain control, the −HG configuration in Table J.6 of its appendix achieves an average realized rate of 0.94 × across five tasks, with success 19 .2

percentage points below the diffusion-policy reference. Neither method accepts a user-specified target rate. These differences in control conditions and rate specification make them less directly aligned with our comparison. RACE similarly changes the low-level controller. It retrains the policy to predict reached states that a higher-gain controller tracks during execution, then applies TOPP-RA (Pham and Pham, 2018) to retime the resulting state trajectory. TOPP-RA computes a time-optimal parameterization of a given geometric path under kinodynamic constraints while preserving its geometry. Applying TOPP-RA in our setting would require specifying how to construct the geometric path, define the kinodynamic constraints, and convert the retimed trajectory into control commands. Furthermore, when the original low-level controller does not tightly track commanded states, deviations between commanded and achieved motion can complicate the tracking of retimed end-effector or joint trajectories. Integrating TOPP-RA would therefore require additional optimization and tracking considerations, as addressed in RACE, and entail broader changes to the execution pipeline than FreeSpeed’s direct action-chunk post-processing, which leaves the underlying control and policy inference unchanged. The remaining methods primarily optimize execution efficiency through autonomous rate selection. Demo-Speedup processes demonstrations through entropy-guided downsampling, retrains the policy, and increases 19 the gripper gain during evaluation. AutoSpeed constructs candidate targets at different rates and trains a Ratio Head to predict the rate. SpeedTuning freezes the base policy but trains an external speed policy through online reinforcement learning to interpolate its action chunks. SpeedAug constructs multi-rate targets through random downsampling and further optimizes the policy through online reinforcement learning. These methods infer or optimize the rate rather than accept it as a user command, so they do not directly support the same analysis of commanded rate, realized rate, and success rate as FreeSpeed. We include SuP as a representative autonomous method because its post-processing of frozen-policy action chunks is most closely related to our mechanism, while TempoVLA provides the comparison in user control. Their differences in training, data processing, and controller settings remain relevant when interpreting the results.

E.4 Out-of-Distribution Measurements in Full 

This section defines the measurement procedure shared by Figures 6, A5, and A6, and presents two additional analyses. 

Observation space. All measurements use the policy’s conditioning representation, consisting of the features produced by the observation encoder and passed to the policy. The Flow Matching policy conditions on eight consecutive observations. Each observation contains six modalities: the language instruction, joint positions, gripper state, end-effector pose, a third-person image, and a wrist image. Each modality is encoded into a 64 -dimensional vector, yielding 384 dimensions per observation. The eight representations are concatenated in temporal order to form a conditioning vector of 8 × 384 = 3072 dimensions. Its final 384 dimensions correspond to the current observation. π0.5 and Fast-WAM take a single observation as input, a setting examined further in Figure A6. 

Training reference set and execution samples. We construct the training reference set from the observations the policy receives in the human demonstrations. For each demonstration, we slide an eight-frame window along the trajectory with a stride of one frame. Each window contains the current frame and the seven preceding frames, matching the observation history used by the Flow Matching policy during execution. We encode each frame using the frozen policy’s encoder and concatenate the eight representations into a 

3072 -dimensional conditioning vector. The conditioning vectors collected across all demonstrations of a task form the training reference set . For Task 60, this set contains 6,176 vectors, shown as the grey cloud in Figures A5 and A6. 25 Execution samples are collected from rollouts. For each execution method, we run 50 rollouts and record the conditioning vector passed to the policy at every replan, yielding one sample per replan. We determine a separate cutoff for each method using the median number of replans required to reach a selected task phase, such as the end of object placement. We then truncate that method’s rollouts at this cutoff. 

Principal-component projection. The projection axes in Figure 6a are determined solely from the training data. We stack the conditioning vectors of all frames in the aforementioned training reference set as rows of an observation feature matrix. We then mean-center this matrix, compute its singular value decomposition (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016), and use the first two right singular vectors as the projection axes. Both the training data and all execution samples are projected onto these axes. For Task 60, the two components explain 31 % and 19 % of the variance, respectively. Different methods produce different numbers of samples. For visualization, the panel displays the same number of points per method, equal to the smallest available sample count. All samples are retained for computing distances and cumulative curves. The projection illustrates the structure of the distributions, while quantitative comparisons are based on the distance measurements. 

Distance to the training reference set. For each execution sample, we compute its Euclidean distances to all training reference vectors in the full conditioning space and average the distances to its k = 5 nearest neighbors (Pari et al., 2021). This gives one distance value per replan. Figure 6b plots the resulting distances for each method as an empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF). At each distance on the horizontal axis, the curve gives the fraction of replans whose distances are at or below that value. A curve further to the left therefore indicates that the method’s conditioning vectors are closer to the training reference set. The reported medians correspond to the 50 % quantile of these distances. The grey dashed curve applies the same measurement to the training reference set itself, excluding self-matches. For each training vector, we retrieve its six nearest neighbors, exclude the vector itself, and average the distances to the remaining five. Figures 6, A5, and A6 display raw distances. In the main text and this section, we also report relative ratios, defined as each method’s median distance divided by that of the 1× reference on the same task. The reference therefore has a relative ratio of 1 by construction. For Task 60, the reference median is 3.290 , and the median for the training set’s self-comparison is 2.121 . At the 2× command, w/o CR, vanilla resampling, and FreeSpeed yield median distances of 5.532 , 5.886 , and 3.569 , corresponding to relative ratios of 1.682 ,

1.789 , and 1.085 , respectively. At 0.3×, vanilla resampling and FreeSpeed yield median distances of 5.532 and 

3.726 , corresponding to relative ratios of 1.680 and 1.132 , respectively. 

Inconsistency and subsequent distribution shift. At each replan, we record the conditioning vector passed to the policy and the Icos of the predicted chunk, computed using Equation (3). Figure 6c pairs the Icos at each replan with the distance to the training reference set at the next replan. This pairing relates chunk inconsistency to the deviation observed after chunk execution, which CI-based rescaling aims to limit. We compute quartile boundaries from the Icos distribution of the 1× reference and apply the same boundaries to all execution methods. For each resulting bin, we report the median distance to the training reference set at the next replan. 

Deceleration. Figure A5 repeats the first two panels of Figure 6 at the 0.3× command. Under deceleration, w/o CR uses ft = ρt and coincides with vanilla resampling, leaving two methods for comparison. The w/o CR ablation yields a median distance of 1.680 times the reference median, while FreeSpeed reduces this to 1.132 .These results show that both acceleration and deceleration can shift the policy’s conditioning inputs away from the training reference set (Römer et al., 2026). Acceleration increases the spacing between consecutive observations along the executed trajectory, whereas deceleration reduces it. FreeSpeed reduces the shift in both cases. 

Single-observation analysis. π0.5 and Fast-WAM take a single observation as input. Figure A6 examines whether the preceding results persist when the measurement is restricted to a single observation. 36 F Limitations 

FreeSpeed cannot achieve every requested rate on every task. Across the four real-robot tasks, increasing the command from 3× to 4× raises the mean realized rate only from 1.62 × to 1.75 ×. The inconsistency-dependent rescaling amplifies action increments more aggressively when consecutive directions remain consistent and brings the factor toward 1 as inconsistency increases. This limits step-length amplification around turns and during critical phases such as approach, grasping, and release, although temporal resampling still applies. The achievable acceleration therefore depends on how much of the task can be executed through directionally consistent motion, resulting in task-dependent limits on the realized speedup. In addition, FreeSpeed determines the rescaling factor solely from the kinematics of the predicted action chunk and does not use tactile or force feedback. Incorporating such physical-interaction signals (Shan et al., 2026) may further guide speed modulation during contact-rich phases. 37
